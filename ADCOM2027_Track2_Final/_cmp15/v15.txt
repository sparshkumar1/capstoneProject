     Authority in Adaptive Technical Interview
 Systems: A Replay-Based Decomposition Study

         Abstract. Adaptive interview-practice systems are often called multi-
         agent even when one controller invokes components procedurally, which
         leaves open whether a behavioral change comes from coordination, from
         specialization, or from the rule that admits proposals. We separate
         these mechanisms in a technical-interview system in which a central
         hub alone commits state. Two rule-based, bounded-authority agents
         (Strategy and Coaching) send typed proposals that are arbitrated
         against a frozen Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) policy. Under
         deterministic replay of 64 constructed answers (8 replay clusters × 20
         question orders = 160 dependent sessions per condition; recorded evalu-
         ator outputs, no live language model, no human candidates), adding the
         agent layer with confidence-gated arbitration changed difficulty trajec-
         tories (level-change difference -2.67, cluster-bootstrap 95% confidence
         interval [-4.50, -0.24]); ablations and a confidence-threshold sweep
         locate the change in the Strategy proposal and its admission, although
         leave-one-cluster-out analysis showed cluster sensitivity. Specialized and
         unified implementations of the same rules produced identical decisions
         on 1,280/1,280 matched open-loop turns, a consistency check on the
         re-implementation, not evidence about specialization generally. In fault
         injection, removing arbitration let adversarial-valid proposals bypass
         guardrails in 38/40 sessions versus 0/40 with it, while a lexical ground-
         ing probe accepted unsupported paraphrased credit in 57/57 applicable
         cases, so containment is mechanism-specific, not semantic. Results rest
         on one frozen PPO checkpoint and replayed stimuli; we make no claim
         about candidate learning, pedagogical efficacy, live-model behavior, or
         the superiority of specialization.

         Keywords: multi-agent coordination · agent specialization · arbitration
         · bounded authority · fault injection · interview practice

1 Introduction

A technical interview evaluates more than whether code passes tests: candidates
explain their reasoning aloud, show conceptual understanding, handle follow-
up questions, and communicate under questioning. A practice system therefore
needs verbal and conceptual coverage, adaptation across turns, and trustwor-
thy feedback, and its decisions accumulate into a trajectory rather than being
independent. A centralized policy simplifies this control loop but couples re-
sponsibilities that may operate at different temporal scales; when they share one
2  Anonymous

decision path, it is hard to tell whether behavior changes arise from the pol-
icy, from the interaction between responsibilities, or from the mechanism that
reconciles competing recommendations.

    Existing solutions address parts of this problem. Coding-practice platforms
such as LeetCode, Codeforces, and HackerRank typically emphasize coding and
problem-solving practice rather than the spoken, adaptive interview interaction
studied here, and general chat systems based on large language models (LLMs)
can generate conversational interview practice or feedback [21]. Research proto-
types use LLMs to give candidates interview feedback [24], to simulate multi-
modal mock technical interviews [10], or to give code feedback [21], and report
case-study or participant-perception evidence (a case study, a qualitative study
with n = 20, a between-subject study with n = 46) on realism, confidence, and
trust.

    These works address portions of the interview-practice problem; this pa-
per asks a different, systems-level question. It is an empirical decomposition
study and does not ask whether one AI interview system works better than an-
other; it asks which architectural mechanism produces an observed behavioral
change when an adaptive technical-interview system is augmented with bounded-
authority agent components. The system has an authoritative session-state hub
and a single-policy reinforcement-learning controller for adaptive difficulty; we
add a layer in which specialized agents keep separate private state and send
typed, constrained proposals, while the hub remains the sole writer of state. The
two added agents are rule- and ledger-based and the hub is centralized; we claim
no new coordination algorithm or decentralized reasoning, only a controlled de-
composition of what such a layer changes. The design separates four properties
often treated together as evidence of a multi-agent design, each with its own
comparison (Table 1): coordination (centralized arbitration of agent proposals
against the existing policy), specialization (division of responsibility and state
across components rather than one unified controller), arbitration (how compet-
ing recommendations are admitted), and bounded authority (which components
may influence committed state and how unsafe proposals are contained). Con-
cretely, M1 versus B0 measures the effect of introducing the agent layer together
with its arbitration, and Msplit versus Munified tests whether role decomposi-
tion alone changes decisions. Without arbitration, adversarial-valid proposals
bypassed guardrails in 38/40 sessions versus 0/40 with it; a lexical grounding
probe accepted unsupported paraphrased credit in 57/57 applicable cases (a
lexical, not semantic, evidence contract); and split and unified implementations
agreed on 1,280/1,280 matched open-loop decisions, a consistency check that
does not establish general equivalence or the absence of a specialization benefit.

    What is tested is replay-level behavioral change, structural consistency of the
split and unified designs, sensitivity to admission thresholds, and containment
of injected faults. What is not tested is human candidates, real-time user adap-
tation, live LLM stochasticity, pedagogical efficacy, universal agent robustness,
and fairness or psychometric properties. The study asks:

2 Related Work

Coordination and specialization. Classical agent-oriented work defines
agency through autonomy, reactivity, pro-activeness, and social ability [22].
LLM-based systems use conversational coordination, as in AutoGen [23], and
specialized roles with standardized workflows, as in MetaGPT [11]. Comparisons
of agent configurations report task-dependent rather than universally superior
arrangements [14], and analyses of multi-agent failures identify specification,
inter-agent alignment, and verification problems [5]. Existing work often evalu-
ates an entire agentic system; this study separates coordination and arbitration
effects from specialization and bounded-authority effects under controlled replay.

    Adaptive sequential decisions. Our system uses PPO [20] as imple-
mented in Stable-Baselines3 [17]. We do not frame the system as multi-agent
reinforcement learning [4]: the two added agents are rule- and ledger-based and
are not trained, and PPO remains a single frozen policy.

    Evaluation and adaptation context. Reinforcement learning for instruc-
tional sequencing [7] and curriculum learning for reinforcement learning [15]
choose which item a learner sees next; we study the decision-layer decomposi-
tion. Sentence-embedding scoring such as Sentence-BERT [18] underlies evalua-
tors like ours, which we do not study as a standalone contribution. LLM-based
4  Anonymous

judging can show position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases [25], moti-
vating future evaluator validation without implying that our evaluator shows
them.

    Safety, shielding, and constrained action. Shielding and supervisory
approaches place external constraints around learned policies so that unsafe
actions are rejected or overridden before they take effect [1, 9].

    Fault injection and resilience. The circuit-breaker pattern, which stops
calling a repeatedly failing dependency [16], serves as one test target. Fault
injection has been applied to LLM-based multi-agent systems [12, 13]; our faults
instead target a rule-based agent layer with explicit authority boundaries.

    Interview-practice systems. Prior interview-practice work [24, 10, 21]
has concentrated on user-facing outcomes; it leaves open whether role separation
changes decisions or only the architecture, as distinct from coordination and
arbitration.

3 System Architecture and Authority Topology

Reference Workflow. Figure 1 shows the application end to end. The Or-
chestrator Agent (the hub) owns one session-state dictionary and one lock. The
QnSelector Agent retrieves a question of the current difficulty from a FAISS vec-
tor store of questions and answers, and the hub shows it through the Interview
UI. The candidate's spoken or typed response returns to the hub, and the Input
Analysis Agent supplies a transcript, behavior metrics, and session-time metrics.
The Transcript Evaluation Agent scores the transcript: a sentence-embedding
similarity S1, a concept-coverage index S2, and a cross-encoder reasoning score
R (0.15 S1 + 0.35 S2eff + 0.50 R plus a bonus term minus a penalty term, with
a cap when a mandatory concept is missed) pass through a Reasoning Critic
and a Validation step that returns the final score, and a strict hand-off con-
tract fails closed on a non-finite or out-of-range score. A separate coding path
(sandboxed execution, code analysis) also returns a final score and is outside the
replay study. The hub commits the turn and logs it. The Strategy/RL Agent
builds the six-dimensional state, and a baseline schedule and a frozen PPO pol-
icy [17, 20] propose an easier/same/harder action, followed by a rule layer of six
guardrails; the resulting next difficulty level returns to the QnSelector Agent.
The follow-up trigger fires if a concept is missing or incorrect, a gap is flagged,
or the score is below 0.80; the Feedback Agent (a local LLM, or a template) then
produces a follow-up question (at most two in a row), and otherwise it returns
detailed personalized feedback. All of this is unchanged when the multi-agent
flag is off, and with Strategy ablated (M2) the difficulty, score, and question
trajectories are identical to B0 (Sect. 6). In Fig. 1, boxes labeled "Agent" are
application modules invoked by the hub and "Strategy/RL Agent" is the PPO
policy; elsewhere, "agent" means the rule-based Strategy and Coaching agents
defined below. The figure labels the cross-encoder's model family; the deployed
weights are fine-tuned, with incomplete provenance. The question bank carries a
coded Bloom level per question (six levels, L1­L6 [3, 2]); it is descriptive meta-
data only, not used for question selection, the RL state or reward, evaluator
targets, or score computation, and no experiment here validates it.

    Specialized Agent Roles and Private State. Table 2 lists what each
agent may propose and may not do. Under least privilege [19], neither agent has
state-write privileges; each receives an immutable, validated observation and re-
turns a typed proposal. They are rule- and ledger-based, not LLM-driven: a
compact design with private persistent state, pro-active proposals, and a com-
munication channel, the properties of weak agency [22] that the existing roles
lacked.

    The matrix fixes what each component may propose (typed, bounded fields)
and what it may not commit; the hub remains the only writer of authoritative
state. The forbidden-key scan blocks proposals that smuggle a state field; stale-
version checks prevent applying a proposal computed against an earlier state;
the grounding check catches well-formed plans that credit a concept the evalua-
tor never found. Schema validation addresses structural invalidity, not semantic
correctness.

    Strategy Agent. It maintains configured difficulty adaptation and tracks
persistent weaknesses within the follow-up budget. Private state: per concept,
the distinct turns in which it was missed or incorrect, later coverage, probes
requested, and an exponential moving average (EMA) of scores. Rules, first
match wins: recover (a low current score with a low recent mean, or a weakness

5 Experimental Methodology

This section describes the dataset, the conditions, the experiments, the param-
eter provenance, and the statistics.

    Evaluation Environment and Replay Dataset. All experiments run
via deterministic replay through the real orchestrator hub, with the evaluator
(the Transcript Evaluation Agent of Fig. 1) replaced by recorded outputs. The
data are 64 constructed (authored) answers to eight technical-interview ques-
tions, scored once by the production evaluator; the 64 payloads were recorded
and replayed, and the evaluator is not re-run. The answers are authored, not an
external public benchmark; the replay uses recorded evaluator outputs and no
human ratings (reference scores from three raters appear only in the exploratory
evaluator analysis below). Each replay cluster is the k-th recorded answer set
across the eight questions (k = 1, . . . , 8); follow-up probes reuse the payload
recorded for the root concept. This is a deterministic replay study of fixed stim-
uli and recorded evaluator outputs, not an evaluation of closed-loop adaptation
Coordination, Specialization, and Bounded Authority in Interview Systems  9

to human candidates. Evidence classes are labeled by experiment: REPLAY
(XA, XC, and the split versus unified comparison), FAULT-INJECTED (XB),
SYNTHETIC (the authored feedback probes of XD and the guardrail state-grid
analysis), and SIMULATED (PPO training against a synthetic candidate model,
evidence about the policy, not about real candidates). Replay sessions combine
the 8 clusters with 20 question orders (seeds 0­19; seed 0 is the original order,
seeds 1­19 apply a seeded shuffle per cluster), yielding N = 160 sessions per
condition. These sessions are not independent: they reuse the same 8 clusters,
which we treat as the independent structural units in cluster-aware analyses. No
live generative LLM is invoked; the LLM gateway is stubbed as down and deter-
ministic template renderers produce text. Per-turn latency was timed in-process
with a monotonic wall-clock timer. Pooled p50 and p95 timings are descriptive;
because replay hardware and operating system were not recorded, absolute val-
ues are not portable benchmarks, latency effects are interpreted through paired
condition differences, and no cluster-level interval was computed for latency.

    Baseline and Ablation Matrix. Experiment XA addresses RQ1 (chiefly
M1 versus B0) and, through its ablations, RQ3 (arbitration) and RQ4 (authority
and grounding safeguards); RQ2 is addressed by the split-versus-unified com-
parison, RQ3 additionally by XC, and RQ4 by XB and XD. XA evaluates the
following conditions across the identical 160 replay sessions:

  ­ B0: frozen single-controller workflow without agents, PPO active, inline
     guardrails. B7: B0 with PPO unavailable (deterministic rule fallback). B0c:
     B0 with the canonical guardrail module.

  ­ M1: full multi-agent system (Strategy and Coaching agents, peer messaging,
     arbitration with  = 0.6). M7: M1 with PPO disabled (paired with B7).
     M1c: M1 with canonical guardrails.

  ­ M2: Strategy Agent ablated (Coaching and the coordination layer remain ac-
     tive). M3: Coaching Agent ablated (Strategy remains active). M6: Strategy-
     to-Coaching peer messaging disabled.

  ­ M4: arbitration and downstream admission-control bypass, so raw Strategy
     proposals are admitted without confidence-threshold, evidence-grounding,
     step-bound, or guardrail-layer checks; because it removes several mechanisms
     at once, it is not a pure arbitration-only ablation. M5: grounding verification
     ablated (Coaching plans accepted without concept-evidence checks). M8:
     authority enforcement ablated (score authority verification bypassed before
     proposal admission).

    Framework for Evaluating Agent Specialization (RQ2). We con-
trast (1) the specialized architecture (Msplit), the two-agent configuration M1
with separate state ledgers and explicit peer messaging, and (2) a unified archi-
tecture (Munified), a monolithic agent with a joint session ledger that synthesizes
difficulty actions, follow-up probes, and coaching plans in a single dispatch. Both
receive identical observations, questions, thresholds, and arbitration rules. Out-
comes are the decision agreement rate (P (Decisionunified = Decisionsplit)), with
difficulty-action and follow-up probe agreement as components, per-turn dis-
patch and serialization latency, and architectural state encapsulation (schema
and ledger boundaries).

    Threshold Decomposition (Experiment XC). Because the bypassed
condition (M4) simultaneously removes the confidence threshold, evidence veri-
fication, and one-step limits, it cannot isolate which mechanism prevents oscil-
lation collapse. XC provides the narrower isolation of the confidence-threshold
mechanism: it sweeps   {0.0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} across the same 160 replay
sessions while holding all other guardrail boundaries and evidence checks active.
Five predictions (C1­C5, Table 8) were pre-specified in the internal protocol
record before any XC run, with  = 0.6 fixed in advance (internal protocol regis-
tration, not external preregistration). The value  = 0.60 is a selected operating
point, not a tuned optimum; it was not tuned against replay outcomes.

    Fault Injection and Circuit Breaker (Experiment XB). To test
whether bounded authority contains component failures (RQ4), constructed
faults were injected into the real agents during replay. Strategy faults were
crash, timeout, malformed output, forbidden state key, stale version, NaN con-
fidence, and adversarial-valid (a well-formed, authorized proposal opposing the
evidence); Coaching faults were crash, malformed output, stale version, and un-
grounded (a valid plan emphasizing a concept absent from the evaluator evi-
dence). Adversarial-valid and ungrounded faults fired on every call; the others
fired with probability 0.3 per agent call (seeded). One pre-specified, exploratory
prediction (B6, Table 8) concerned the per-agent circuit breaker opening at least
once in at least 80% of full-path crash, malformed, stale, and NaN-confidence
sessions. Each fault was crossed with four variants (full path, authority check
removed, arbitration removed, grounding check removed) over 40 sessions per
cell (8 clusters × 5 order seeds) with a 150 ms agent timeout. A fault counted as
contained only if the session completed, committed scores equaled the evaluator
output, and every difficulty transition was valid; guardrail bypass, ungrounded-
concept leakage, and breaker opening were counted per session. Each variant
removes a mechanism and then injects the fault it guards against, so results
show the necessity of a mechanism for the tested property, not general fault
tolerance.

    Grounding Probe (Experiment XD). Separately from the proposal-
level concept-grounding check, the deployed lexical feedback validator was
probed with authored (synthetic) feedback texts; no LLM was involved. For
each of the 64 recorded evaluator payloads, seven constructed feedback cat-
egories were checked (for example faithful text, a score leak, and truncated
paraphrase credit, which credits a concept in paraphrase without supporting
evidence). Placeholder concept texts were relabeled uniquely to remove an
evidence artifact, and a category was counted only for payloads where it could
be constructed.

result from any other checkpoint is used. Training was SIMULATED: a single

synthetic candidate (fixed skill  0.6, one persona, skill constant over time)
generated scores, confidence, and hesitation for the six-dimensional state, with

    The cross-encoder is called through the sentence-transformers predict in-

    Statistical Methodology. For session-level point estimates and paired
differences, we compute standard non-parametric percentile bootstrap intervals
with B = 10,000 resamples. For bounded completion, transition, and invariant
proportions, we report exact Clopper­Pearson binomial confidence intervals [6].
To account for the 8-cluster dependency, we implement a cluster bootstrap over

7 Discussion

Admission of agent proposals changes behavior; role splitting pre-
served it. Under the tested replay conditions, introducing the agent layer
altered the difficulty trajectory relative to the frozen baseline (B0), whereas reor-
ganizing identical responsibilities into specialized versus unified components did
not alter matched per-turn decisions. This is consistent with the change aris-
ing from the introduction and admission of auxiliary recommendations, while
specialization reorganizes responsibility and state boundaries. The split-versus-
unified result is a consistency check on a re-implementation of the same rules,
not evidence that specialization has or lacks value in general.

    Arbitration as the control point. Ablating Strategy (M2) reproduced
the baseline, whereas removing Coaching (M3) or the peer message (M6) left
the trajectory unchanged: Strategy influenced difficulty only through the ar-
bitration/admission pathway, since neither agent has state-write authority, and

Coaching has no authority over difficulty. Removing arbitration and downstream
admission control (M4) collapsed the trajectory, and the threshold sweep indi-

the confidence gate, and the agent structure together; the ablations (M2, M4,
XC) place the change in the Strategy proposal and its admission, but the study
cannot separate the added rules from an agent layer. PPO has little influence
here: disabling it changed 4 of 160 sessions (A9).

    Lower volatility is not better adaptation. M1 showed fewer level
changes and reversals than B0 (Table 6), but removing arbitration gave an even
flatter trajectory while abandoning PPO alignment and allowing bypass. Stabil-
ity and adaptation are distinct objectives, and stability is a systems metric, not
a pedagogical criterion.

    Authority and containment. Fault injection supports a narrow contain-
ment claim under the tested fault model: schema validation, forbidden-key scan-
ning, and version checks intercepted malformed, unauthorized, and stale propos-
als; grounding addressed unsupported attribution; the breaker handled repeated
failures; and arbitration contained adversarial-valid proposals. This is a layered
safeguard picture, not a proof of general safety, and structural or lexical valida-
tion does not establish semantic correctness.

    Design lessons from this case study. (i) Separate proposal generation
from authoritative state mutation (Tables 2 and 9). (ii) In the tested setting,
central arbitration was more behaviorally consequential than role splitting (Ta-
bles 6 and 5). Role splitting could not change decisions here, because the unified
controller applies the same rules. (iii) Safety controls should operate downstream
of proposal generation, and overlapping safeguards cannot be told apart without
ablation (Tables 9, 10, 5). (iv) Different fault classes need different safeguards
(Tables 9 and 10). (v) Negative specialization results are useful: organization
alone produced no behavioral benefit here (Sect. 6); the check shows that reor-
ganizing preserved behavior, not that a benefit is absent.

9 Conclusion

This study asked whether any change in the decisions of an adaptive technical-
interview system stems from specialization or from coordination and arbitration.
Across 160 deterministic replay sessions per condition, adding the agent layer
with confidence-gated arbitration altered difficulty trajectories relative to the
frozen single-policy baseline, although the magnitude was sensitive to the eight
replay clusters. A matched open-loop comparison of the specialized and unified
designs produced identical decisions on all 1,280 turns, a consistency check on the
re-implementation: under the tested replay conditions, role splitting preserved
behavior. Bounded-authority mechanisms gave mechanism-specific containment
of the evaluated injected faults.

    The evaluation does not establish human-candidate efficacy, pedagogical ben-
efit, online candidate learning, equivalence for live stochastic LLM agents, gen-
eral semantic correctness, universal fault tolerance, or general superiority of spe-
cialization or PPO. Specialization structures responsibility and private state,
whereas coordination and arbitration determine which proposal becomes au-
thoritative. Future work should test these boundaries across policy checkpoints,
human candidates, and generative multi-agent architectures (Sect. 8).
