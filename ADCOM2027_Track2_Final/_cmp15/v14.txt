         Abstract. Adaptive interview-practice systems are often called multi-
         agent even when one controller invokes components procedurally, which
         leaves open whether a behavioral change comes from coordination, from
         specialization, or from the rule that admits proposals. We separate these
         mechanisms in a technical-interview system in which a central hub alone
         commits state. Two rule-based, bounded-authority agents (Strategy and
         Coaching) send typed proposals that are arbitrated against a frozen Prox-
         imal Policy Optimization (PPO) policy. Under deterministic replay of 64
         constructed answers (8 replay clusters × 20 question orders = 160 depen-
         dent sessions per condition; recorded evaluator outputs, no live language
         model, no human candidates), adding the agent layer with confidence-
         gated arbitration changed difficulty trajectories (level-change difference
         -2.67, cluster-bootstrap 95% confidence interval [-4.50, -0.24]); abla-
         tions and a confidence-threshold sweep locate the change in the Strat-
         egy proposal and its admission, although leave-one-cluster-out analysis
         showed cluster sensitivity. Specialized and unified implementations of the
         same rules produced identical decisions on 1,280/1,280 matched open-
         loop turns, a consistency check on the re-implementation, not evidence
         about specialization generally. In fault injection, removing arbitration
         let adversarial-valid proposals bypass guardrails in 38/40 sessions versus
         0/40 with it, while a lexical grounding probe accepted unsupported para-
         phrased credit in 57/57 applicable cases, so containment is mechanism-
         specific, not semantic. Results rest on one frozen PPO checkpoint and
         replayed stimuli; we make no claim about candidate learning, pedagogi-
         cal efficacy, live-model behavior, or the superiority of specialization.

         Keywords: multi-agent coordination · agent specialization · arbitra-
         tion · bounded authority · fault injection · interview practice

1 Introduction

A technical interview evaluates more than whether code passes tests: candidates
explain their reasoning aloud, show conceptual understanding, handle follow-
up questions, and communicate under questioning. A practice system therefore
needs verbal and conceptual coverage, adaptation across turns, and trustwor-
thy feedback, and its decisions accumulate into a trajectory rather than being
independent. A centralized policy simplifies this control loop but couples respon-
sibilities that may operate at different temporal scales; when they share one
2  Anonymous

  decision path, it is hard to tell whether behavior changes arise from the pol-
  icy, from the interaction between responsibilities, or from the mechanism that
  reconciles competing recommendations.

      Existing solutions address parts of this problem. Coding-practice platforms
  such as LeetCode, Codeforces, and HackerRank typically emphasize coding and
  problem-solving practice rather than the spoken, adaptive interview interaction
  studied here, and general chat systems based on large language models (LLMs)
  can generate conversational interview practice or feedback [21]. Research proto-
  types use LLMs to give candidates interview feedback [24], to simulate multi-
  modal mock technical interviews [10], or to give code feedback [21], and report
  case-study or participant-perception evidence (a case study, a qualitative study
  with n = 20, a between-subject study with n = 46) on realism, confidence, and
  trust.

      These works address portions of the interview-practice problem; this pa-
  per asks a different, systems-level question. It is an empirical decomposition
  study and does not ask whether one AI interview system works better than an-
  other; it asks which architectural mechanism produces an observed behavioral
  change when an adaptive technical-interview system is augmented with bounded-
  authority agent components. The system has an authoritative session-state hub
  and a single-policy reinforcement-learning controller for adaptive difficulty; we
  add a layer in which specialized agents keep separate private state and send
  typed, constrained proposals, while the hub remains the sole writer of state.
  The design separates four properties often treated together as evidence of a
  multi-agent design, each with its own comparison (Table 1): coordination (cen-
  tralized arbitration of agent proposals against the existing policy), specialization
  (division of responsibility and state across components rather than one unified
  controller), arbitration (how competing recommendations are admitted), and
  bounded authority (which components may influence committed state and how
  unsafe proposals are contained). Concretely, M1 versus B0 measures the effect
  of introducing the agent layer together with its arbitration, and Msplit versus
  Munified tests whether role decomposition alone changes decisions. Without arbi-
  tration, adversarial-valid proposals bypassed guardrails in 38/40 sessions versus
  0/40 with it; a lexical grounding probe accepted unsupported paraphrased credit
  in 57/57 applicable cases (a lexical, not semantic, evidence contract); and split
  and unified implementations agreed on 1,280/1,280 matched open-loop decisions,
  a consistency check that does not establish general equivalence or the absence
  of a specialization benefit.

      What is tested is replay-level behavioral change, structural consistency of the
  split and unified designs, sensitivity to admission thresholds, and containment
  of injected faults. What is not tested is human candidates, real-time user adap-
  tation, live LLM stochasticity, pedagogical efficacy, universal agent robustness,
  and fairness or psychometric properties. The study asks:

2 Related Work

Coordination and specialization. Classical agent-oriented work defines
agency through autonomy, reactivity, pro-activeness, and social ability [22].
LLM-based systems use conversational coordination, as in AutoGen [23], and
specialized roles with standardized workflows, as in MetaGPT [11]. Comparisons
of agent configurations report task-dependent rather than universally superior
arrangements [14], and analyses of multi-agent failures identify specification,
inter-agent alignment, and verification problems [5]. Existing work often evalu-
ates an entire agentic system; this study separates coordination and arbitration
effects from specialization and bounded-authority effects under controlled replay.

    Adaptive sequential decisions. Our system uses PPO [20] as implemented
in Stable-Baselines3 [17]. We do not frame the system as multi-agent reinforce-
ment learning [4]: the two added agents are rule- and ledger-based and are not
trained, and PPO remains a single frozen policy.

    Evaluation and adaptation context. Reinforcement learning for instruc-
tional sequencing [7] and curriculum learning for reinforcement learning [15]
choose which item a learner sees next; we study the decision-layer decomposi-
tion. Sentence-embedding scoring such as Sentence-BERT [18] underlies evalua-
tors like ours, which we do not study as a standalone contribution. LLM-based
4  Anonymous

judging can show position, verbosity, and self-enhancement biases [25], motivat-
ing future evaluator validation without implying that our evaluator shows them.

    Safety, shielding, and constrained action. Shielding and supervisory
approaches place external constraints around learned policies so that unsafe
actions are rejected or overridden before they take effect [1, 9].

    Fault injection and resilience. The circuit-breaker pattern, which stops
calling a repeatedly failing dependency [16], serves as one test target. Fault
injection has been applied to LLM-based multi-agent systems [12, 13]; our faults
instead target a rule-based agent layer with explicit authority boundaries.

    Interview-practice systems. Prior interview-practice work [24, 10, 21] has
concentrated on user-facing outcomes; it leaves open whether role separation
changes decisions or only the architecture, as distinct from coordination and
arbitration.

3 System Architecture and Authority Topology

Reference Workflow. Figure 1 shows the application end to end. The Or-
chestrator Agent (the hub) owns one session-state dictionary and one lock. The
QnSelector Agent retrieves a question of the current difficulty from a FAISS vec-
tor store of questions and answers, and the hub shows it through the Interview
UI. The candidate's spoken or typed response returns to the hub, and the Input
Analysis Agent supplies a transcript, behavior metrics, and session-time metrics.
The Transcript Evaluation Agent scores the transcript: a sentence-embedding
similarity S1, a concept-coverage index S2, and a cross-encoder reasoning score
R (0.15 S1 + 0.35 S2eff + 0.50 R plus a bonus term minus a penalty term, with
a cap when a mandatory concept is missed) pass through a Reasoning Critic
and a Validation step that returns the final score, and a strict hand-off con-
tract fails closed on a non-finite or out-of-range score. A separate coding path
(sandboxed execution, code analysis) also returns a final score and is outside the
replay study. The hub commits the turn and logs it. The Strategy/RL Agent
builds the six-dimensional state, and a baseline schedule and a frozen PPO pol-
icy [17, 20] propose an easier/same/harder action, followed by a rule layer of six
guardrails; the resulting next difficulty level returns to the QnSelector Agent.
The follow-up trigger fires if a concept is missing or incorrect, a gap is flagged,
or the score is below 0.80; the Feedback Agent (a local LLM, or a template) then
produces a follow-up question (at most two in a row), and otherwise it returns
detailed personalized feedback. All of this is unchanged when the multi-agent
flag is off, and with Strategy ablated (M2) the difficulty, score, and question
trajectories are identical to B0 (Sect. 6). In Fig. 1, boxes labeled "Agent" are
application modules invoked by the hub and "Strategy/RL Agent" is the PPO
policy; elsewhere, "agent" means the rule-based Strategy and Coaching agents
defined below. The figure labels the cross-encoder's model family; the deployed
weights are fine-tuned, with incomplete provenance. The question bank carries
a coded Bloom level per question (six levels, L1­L6 [3, 2]); it is descriptive meta-
data only, not used for question selection, the RL state or reward, evaluator
targets, or score computation, and no experiment here validates it.

    Three kinds of state. Proposal state is an agent's typed output plus its
private ledger; authoritative state (committed scores, difficulty level, question
queue, follow-up counters, state version) is written only by the hub and persisted
in SQLite; the control/admission layer between them comprises the schema,
forbidden-key, version, grounding, confidence-gate, step-bound, and guardrail
checks.

    Specialized Agent Roles and Private State. Table 2 lists what each
agent may propose and may not do. Under least privilege [19], neither agent has
state-write privileges; each receives an immutable, validated observation and re-
turns a typed proposal. They are rule- and ledger-based, not LLM-driven: a
compact design with private persistent state, pro-active proposals, and a com-
munication channel, the properties of weak agency [22] that the existing roles
lacked.

    The matrix fixes what each component may propose (typed, bounded fields)
and what it may not commit; the hub remains the only writer of authoritative
state. The forbidden-key scan blocks proposals that smuggle a state field; stale-
version checks prevent applying a proposal computed against an earlier state;
the grounding check catches well-formed plans that credit a concept the evalua-
tor never found. Schema validation addresses structural invalidity, not semantic
correctness.

decision is a typed object (unknown fields, NaN, and out-of-range values re-

state version, and provenance, and every accept, reject, or modify outcome with
a reason code. Optimistic concurrency uses a state version that counts commit-
ted turns: a proposal whose version, turn, or session differs from the hub's is stale
and dropped, and a second answer from the same agent for the same version is
a duplicate and dropped.

    PPO integration. PPO keeps its role and its 6-D state and 3-action defini-
tions; the policy was trained once and frozen before the multi-agent evaluation
(Sect. 5), without hyperparameter search or result-based checkpoint selection.
Its action is one input to arbitration; the agents never see it before proposing,
and we make no claim that they improve PPO.

    Guardrails. The deployed hub uses an inline copy of the rule layer that
differs from the training environment's module; a configuration key selects the
deployed (legacy inline) or canonical implementation, and both are reported.
On a synthetic grid (39,930 points, hesitation = 1- acoustic confidence) the two
disagree on 4,728 points (11.8%); this is a property of the grid, not a deployment
frequency.

    Proposal Validation. Before any proposal reaches arbitration, the coordi-
nator executes four sequential checks: (1) strict schema validation; (2) recursive
scanning against 18 forbidden state keys; (3) optimistic concurrency verification
against the hub's committed turn version; and (4) concept evidence grounding.
If an agent raises an unhandled exception or exceeds its timeout (2 s in XA and
XC, 150 ms in XB), its proposal is dropped and the hub proceeds with the de-
fault policy; a per-agent circuit breaker opens after two consecutive failures with
a 60 s cooldown.

5 Experimental Methodology

This section describes the dataset, the conditions, the experiments, the param-
eter provenance, and the statistics. Evaluation Environment and Replay
Dataset. All experiments run via deterministic replay through the real orches-
trator hub, with the evaluator (the Transcript Evaluation Agent of Fig. 1) re-
placed by recorded outputs. The data are 64 constructed (authored) answers to
eight technical-interview questions, scored once by the production evaluator; the
64 payloads were recorded and replayed, and the evaluator is not re-run. The an-
swers are authored, not an external public benchmark; the replay uses recorded
evaluator outputs and no human ratings (reference scores from three raters ap-
pear only in the exploratory evaluator analysis below). Each replay cluster is
the k-th recorded answer set across the eight questions (k = 1, . . . , 8); follow-up
probes reuse the payload recorded for the root concept. This is a deterministic
replay study of fixed stimuli and recorded evaluator outputs, not an evaluation
of closed-loop adaptation to human candidates. Evidence classes are labeled
by experiment: REPLAY (XA, XC, and the split versus unified comparison),
FAULT-INJECTED (XB), SYNTHETIC (the authored feedback probes of XD
and the guardrail state-grid analysis), and SIMULATED (PPO training against
a synthetic candidate model, evidence about the policy, not about real candi-
dates). Replay sessions combine the 8 clusters with 20 question orders (seeds
0­19; seed 0 is the original order, seeds 1­19 apply a seeded shuffle per cluster),
yielding N = 160 sessions per condition. These sessions are not independent:
they reuse the same 8 clusters, which we treat as the independent structural
units in cluster-aware analyses. No live generative LLM is invoked; the LLM
gateway is stubbed as down and deterministic template renderers produce text.
Per-turn latency was timed in-process with a monotonic wall-clock timer. Pooled
p50 and p95 timings are descriptive; because replay hardware and operating sys-
tem were not recorded, absolute values are not portable benchmarks, latency
effects are interpreted through paired condition differences, and no cluster-level
interval was computed for latency.

    Framework for Evaluating Agent Specialization (RQ2). We contrast
(1) the specialized architecture (Msplit), the two-agent configuration M1 with
separate state ledgers and explicit peer messaging, and (2) a unified architecture
(Munified), a monolithic agent with a joint session ledger that synthesizes difficulty
actions, follow-up probes, and coaching plans in a single dispatch. Both receive
identical observations, questions, thresholds, and arbitration rules. Outcomes are
the decision agreement rate (P (Decisionunified = Decisionsplit)), with difficulty-
action and follow-up probe agreement as components, per-turn dispatch and
serialization latency, and architectural state encapsulation (schema and ledger
boundaries).

    Threshold Decomposition (Experiment XC). Because the bypassed
condition (M4) simultaneously removes the confidence threshold, evidence veri-
fication, and one-step limits, it cannot isolate which mechanism prevents oscil-
lation collapse. XC provides the narrower isolation of the confidence-threshold
mechanism: it sweeps   {0.0, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} across the same 160 replay
sessions while holding all other guardrail boundaries and evidence checks active.
Five predictions (C1­C5, Table 8) were pre-specified in the internal protocol
record before any XC run, with  = 0.6 fixed in advance (internal protocol regis-
tration, not external preregistration). The value  = 0.60 is a selected operating
point, not a tuned optimum; it was not tuned against replay outcomes.

    Fault Injection and Circuit Breaker (Experiment XB). To test
whether bounded authority contains component failures (RQ4), constructed
faults were injected into the real agents during replay. Strategy faults were
crash, timeout, malformed output, forbidden state key, stale version, NaN
10  Anonymous

confidence, and adversarial-valid (a well-formed, authorized proposal opposing
the evidence); Coaching faults were crash, malformed output, stale version, and
ungrounded (a valid plan emphasizing a concept absent from the evaluator
evidence). Adversarial-valid and ungrounded faults fired on every call; the
others fired with probability 0.3 per agent call (seeded). One pre-specified,
exploratory prediction (B6, Table 8) concerned the per-agent circuit breaker
opening at least once in at least 80% of full-path crash, malformed, stale, and
NaN-confidence sessions. Each fault was crossed with four variants (full path,
authority check removed, arbitration removed, grounding check removed) over
40 sessions per cell (8 clusters × 5 order seeds) with a 150 ms agent timeout.
A fault counted as contained only if the session completed, committed scores
equaled the evaluator output, and every difficulty transition was valid; guardrail
bypass, ungrounded-concept leakage, and breaker opening were counted per
session. Each variant removes a mechanism and then injects the fault it guards
against, so results show the necessity of a mechanism for the tested property,
not general fault tolerance.

    Grounding Probe (Experiment XD). Separately from the proposal-level
concept-grounding check, the deployed lexical feedback validator was probed
with authored (synthetic) feedback texts; no LLM was involved. For each of
the 64 recorded evaluator payloads, seven constructed feedback categories were
checked (for example faithful text, a score leak, and truncated paraphrase credit,
which credits a concept in paraphrase without supporting evidence). Placeholder
concept texts were relabeled uniquely to remove an evidence artifact, and a
category was counted only for payloads where it could be constructed.

    Provenance and Reproducibility. Table 4 records how each parameter

    Statistical Methodology. For session-level point estimates and paired dif-
ferences, we compute standard non-parametric percentile bootstrap intervals
with B = 10,000 resamples. For bounded completion, transition, and invari-
ant proportions, we report exact Clopper­Pearson binomial confidence intervals
[6]. To account for the 8-cluster dependency, we implement a cluster bootstrap
over the 8 underlying replay clusters following Field and Welsh [8] (B = 10,000),
resampling entire replay clusters with replacement. We also report a leave-one-
cluster-out (LOCO) sensitivity analysis. All intervals are descriptive; no signifi-
cance tests or p-values are computed.

7 Discussion

Admission of agent proposals changes behavior; role splitting pre-
served it. Under the tested replay conditions, introducing the agent layer altered
the difficulty trajectory relative to the frozen baseline (B0), whereas reorganizing
identical responsibilities into specialized versus unified components did not alter
matched per-turn decisions. This is consistent with the change arising from the
introduction and admission of auxiliary recommendations, while specialization
reorganizes responsibility and state boundaries. The split-versus-unified result is
a consistency check on a re-implementation of the same rules, not evidence that
specialization has or lacks value in general.

    Arbitration as the control point. Ablating Strategy (M2) reproduced
the baseline, whereas removing Coaching (M3) or the peer message (M6) left
the trajectory unchanged: Strategy influenced difficulty only through the ar-
bitration/admission pathway, since neither agent has state-write authority, and
Coaching has no authority over difficulty. Removing arbitration and downstream
admission control (M4) collapsed the trajectory, and the threshold sweep indi-
cated that  controls proposal admission. M1 versus B0 adds the Strategy rules,
the confidence gate, and the agent structure together; the ablations (M2, M4,
XC) place the change in the Strategy proposal and its admission, but the study
cannot separate the added rules from an agent layer. PPO has little influence
here: disabling it changed 4 of 160 sessions (A9).

    Lower volatility is not better adaptation. M1 showed fewer level
changes and reversals than B0 (Table 6), but removing arbitration gave an
even flatter trajectory while abandoning PPO alignment and allowing bypass.
Stability and adaptation are distinct objectives, and stability is a systems
metric, not a pedagogical criterion.

    Authority and containment. Fault injection supports a narrow contain-
ment claim under the tested fault model: schema validation, forbidden-key scan-
ning, and version checks intercepted malformed, unauthorized, and stale propos-
als; grounding addressed unsupported attribution; the breaker handled repeated
failures; and arbitration contained adversarial-valid proposals. This is a layered
safeguard picture, not a proof of general safety, and structural or lexical valida-
tion does not establish semantic correctness.

    Design lessons from this case study. (i) Separate proposal generation
from authoritative state mutation (Tables 2 and 9). (ii) In the tested setting, cen-
tral arbitration was more behaviorally consequential than role splitting (Tables 6
and 5). (iii) Safety controls should operate downstream of proposal generation,
and overlapping safeguards cannot be told apart without ablation (Tables 9,
10, 5). (iv) Different fault classes need different safeguards (Tables 9 and 10).
(v) Negative specialization results are useful: organization alone produced no
behavioral benefit here (Sect. 6).

9 Conclusion

This study asked whether any change in the decisions of an adaptive technical-
interview system stems from specialization or from coordination and arbitration.
Across 160 deterministic replay sessions per condition, adding the agent layer
with confidence-gated arbitration altered difficulty trajectories relative to the
frozen single-policy baseline, although the magnitude was sensitive to the eight
replay clusters. A matched open-loop comparison of the specialized and unified
designs produced identical decisions on all 1,280 turns, a consistency check on the
re-implementation: under the tested replay conditions, role splitting preserved
behavior. Bounded-authority mechanisms gave mechanism-specific containment
of the evaluated injected faults.

    The evaluation does not establish human-candidate efficacy, pedagogical ben-
efit, online candidate learning, equivalence for live stochastic LLM agents, gen-
eral semantic correctness, universal fault tolerance, or general superiority of spe-
cialization or PPO. Specialization structures responsibility and private state,
whereas coordination and arbitration determine which proposal becomes au-
thoritative. Future work should test these boundaries across policy checkpoints,
human candidates, and generative multi-agent architectures (Sect. 8).
