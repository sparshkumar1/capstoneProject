# Synthetic Category-Discrimination Benchmark for the Hybrid Evaluator — V13 (final)

**Status: complete protocol, NOT executed, NOT registered.** V1–V12 preserved unmodified, sample and design untouched. This version corrects a real mathematical overreach in V12 §1 (the compound-test bound was applied more broadly, and described as tighter, than the actual theorem supports), removes an unjustified "floor" characterization of real-data calibration (V12 §2), and adds a threshold-level gap that had gone unnoticed (§0 below). ## 0. A gap not previously noticed: the simulation checked only the 0.05 threshold, not 0.025

**Checked directly, as instructed.** The Holm step-down (V6 §2.3) compares the smaller of `{p_P1, p_P2}` to 0.025 and the larger to 0.05 — two different thresholds. The saved calibration script's rejection rule is hardcoded as `p_value <= 0.05` (`d1_bootstrap_calibration_check.py` line 44); it was never run at 0.025. A percentile bootstrap's miscalibration is not guaranteed to scale proportionally across thresholds — a test that rejects at ~6–7% against a nominal 5% ceiling could plausibly be closer to, father from, or even on the other side of its nominal rate at a 2.5% ceiling; nothing in the current evidence says which. **Corrected scope:** the ~6–7% figure characterizes behavior at the 0.05 threshold only. No claim is made, and none should be inferred, about calibration at 0.025 — that comparison, used for whichever of `p_P1`/`p_P2` is smaller in the actual Holm step-down, remains entirely unchecked.

## 1. The compound-test (IUT) bound — corrected to state precisely what it does and does not cover

**P1's composite null, written out exactly.** P1 combines P1-i and P1-ii via `p_P1 = max(p_{P1-i}, p_{P1-ii})` (V6 §2.1), an intersection–union test (IUT): P1 is rejected only when *both* component nulls are rejected. Consequently P1's **composite null is the union** of the two component nulls, `H0_P1 = H0_{P1-i} ∪ H0_{P1-ii}` — true whenever *at least one* of the two component effects is absent, including configurations where one component's effect is exactly zero and the other is strongly present. **P2's composite null has the identical union structure**, by the same max-p construction over its two one-sided TOST components (`p_P2 = max(p_upper, p_lower)`): P2's null is "the true difference lies outside the equivalence margin on at least one side," rejected only when both one-sided nulls are rejected.

**Checking the claimed bound against the full union null, per the instruction.** The inequality `P(A∩B) ≤ min(P(A),P(B))` is true unconditionally, for any two events, regardless of dependence — that part of V12 §1 is correct and needs no change. The classical intersection–union-test result (Berger, 1982; Berger & Hsu, 1996) that licenses using it here is: **if every component test is individually level-α across its own entire null parameter space**, then at any point θ in the union null, at least one component's null is true there, and the compound (all-must-reject) test's rejection probability at θ is bounded by that one component's rejection probability at θ, which is ≤ α by that component's own level-α property — *regardless of the other component's parameter value*, including a strongly-alternative one. This holds at every corner of the union null, including the "one null / one strongly alternative" configuration the instruction asks about, **provided** the level-α property of the null-true component was verified *at that specific parameter configuration*, not merely at one convenient point.

**This is exactly where V12 overstated the evidence.** The calibration simulation (§0 of V12, unchanged here) evaluated exactly one scenario per component test: the joint configuration where the *tested* quantity's true effect is zero, with only nuisance between-topic heterogeneity (τ) varied. It never simulated a configuration in which one component's null holds while the *other* component's true effect is large. Because P1-i and P1-ii (and P2's two one-sided components) are computed from overlapping, correlated data — the same constructed-answer score realizations feeding both the raw superiority contrast and the interaction/dampening contrast — it is not automatically true that a component's own Type-I rate stays near the simulated ~6–7% when its companion component is far into its alternative; a strong dampening effect, for instance, could shift the joint score distribution in a way that also perturbs the marginal null distribution of the other component's statistic. **This has not been checked in either direction.** The correct, narrower statement is:

- The bound `P(reject P1) ≤ (component Type-I rate)` is verified to hold **at the one null configuration actually simulated** — both components simultaneously at their own null, at 0.05.
- Extending that bound to **every** point of the union null (in particular, the "one null / one strongly alternative" corners) additionally requires each component's calibration to be stable against variation in the *other* component's parameter — an assumption that is plausible given the shared IUT logic, but is **not itself established by anything simulated here**, and is not asserted as established.

**The "generally strictly lower" claim in V12 §1 is withdrawn as stated, and corrected.** V12 asserted the compound rate is "in general strictly lower [than the individual rate], since equality only holds in the degenerate case of perfect positive correlation." This gets the least-favorable case backwards. The IUT literature's own worst-case analysis is precisely the configuration this instruction asked to check: one component exactly at its null boundary, the other component's true effect large enough that its test rejects with power near 1. In that configuration, `P(reject both) = P(reject null-true component) × P(reject other | reject null-true component) ≈ P(reject null-true component)` whenever the near-certain rejection of the alternative-true component is not negatively associated with the null-true component's rejection — i.e., the bound is **approached, not strictly beaten**, exactly in the realistic case where one effect is real and large and the other is absent, which is not a "degenerate" curiosity but the ordinary shape of a partial-null configuration. **Corrected statement:** the compound test's Type-I error is bounded above by whichever component's Type-I rate applies at the true (unknown) null configuration; that bound can be approached closely, not just in contrived edge cases; nothing here supports a general claim of strict, comfortable slack below the ~6–7% figure.

## 2. Calibration interpretation — the "floor" claim removed; 0.025 explicitly out of scope

**V12 §2 claimed** the simulated ~6–7% figures are "a floor on the concern... not a ceiling," i.e., that real-data calibration is guaranteed to be at least this bad, possibly worse. **This is not established and is removed.** The intuition — that imposing exchangeability by construction represents a best case, and that violating it *could* make things worse — remains a reasonable, disclosed concern, but "could be worse" is not the same as "is at least this bad": there is no theorem here showing that non-exchangeable real topics monotonically degrade this specific bootstrap's calibration relative to the idealized simulation; in principle a different departure from the simulated model could leave calibration unchanged or even improve it in some configuration. **Corrected statement:** the simulated ~6–7% figure describes the bootstrap's behavior under an idealized, exchangeability-imposed model of D1's exact cluster-size structure, at the 0.05 threshold, for a single component test in isolation. It is **not shown to be, and is not claimed to be, either a floor or a ceiling on the real confirmatory run's actual Type-I error** — it is simply the best currently-available evidence about this procedure's mechanics, under stated assumptions that may or may not hold for the real data, at one of the two thresholds Holm actually uses.

## 3. Holm — the mathematical guarantee kept exact; no quantified FWER claim, qualitative or otherwise

**Holm's theorem, restated precisely and left untouched:** if each of `p_P1` and `p_P2` is marginally valid (stochastically no smaller than Uniform(0,1) under its own null) at the specific comparison threshold used (0.025 for the smaller, 0.05 for the larger), then the Holm procedure's family-wise error rate is ≤ the nominal 0.05, as a mathematical fact independent of this design's specifics. **What is and is not currently known about whether that precondition holds here:** §1 establishes the IUT structure correctly bounds each compound p-value's validity *at the one joint-null configuration simulated, at the 0.05 threshold* — it does not establish validity at 0.025 (§0), and does not establish validity uniformly across the full union null space, including the "one null/one strongly alternative" corners (§1). **V12's remaining qualitative claim — that Holm's actual family-wise rate is "at least as good as, and generally better than, the individual-component finding suggests" — is withdrawn here as well**, for the same reason as §1's "generally strictly lower" claim: it assumed a slack that is not established and can be approached with near-equality in realistic configurations. **The corrected position:** the *procedural* guarantee of Holm — a pre-specified, disclosed decision rule applied identically regardless of which result looks more favorable — is unconditional and unaffected by any of this (unchanged from V10 §3/V11 §4, restated here). The *numerical* family-wise error rate is not quantified, not bounded by a specific percentage, and not qualitatively characterized as better or worse than the single-test finding — it is stated as genuinely open, pending either (a) a direct joint simulation of the full two-test Holm procedure (named in V12 §6 as a possible future step, still not undertaken), or (b) acceptance of the procedure's pre-registration value alone, independent of an exact FWER number.
