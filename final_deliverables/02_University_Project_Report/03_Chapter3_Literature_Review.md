# Chapter 3: Literature Review

The capstone project's own development drew on literature spanning automated interview evaluation, LLM-based automated scoring, LLM reliability/verification, multi-agent coordination benchmarking, and multimodal signal fusion in reinforcement learning. The six citations originally assembled for this survey (`revised_report_v5.pdf`, Ch.2) were independently re-verified via live search in this phase, since `CLAUDE.md`'s citation-integrity rule requires every reference to trace to a verified source, not be assumed accurate from a prior draft. **Verification status is reported transparently below — this is itself part of this phase's evidence discipline, not a cosmetic citation list.**

## 3.1 Automated Interview Evaluation Systems

**Status: resolved (2026-11-13).** The originally cited "Wu et al. (2026)" description (an AI-driven interview evaluation system coordinating multiple agents across voice, facial expression, and text signals via virtual digital-human avatars) could not be matched to a specific, exactly-corresponding paper via live search, in this phase or on a fresh independent re-check. It is replaced with **CoMAI** [6] — Sun, Yu, Yin, Yang, Zhang, and Xu, *"CoMAI: A Collaborative Multi-Agent Framework for Robust and Equitable Interview Evaluation"* (arXiv:2603.16215, 2026) — a verified, closely-related real paper: a multi-agent interview-evaluation system coordinated by a centralized finite-state machine, independently confirmed (live search, 2026-11-13) to report 90.47% assessment accuracy (a 30.47-point improvement over single-agent baselines and 19.05 points over human interviewers), 83.33% recall, and a 100% defense success rate against 500+ adversarial attacks in its own evaluation. PrepAIred differs from this line of work: it evaluates the *substantive correctness* of an answer's content via a rubric-anchored semantic pipeline, not primarily behavioural/delivery signals, and is a single-controller system, not a multi-agent one.

## 3.2 LLM-Based Automated Scoring and Grading

**Status: both citations confirmed real and accurately described.**

- **Karjus et al. (2026)**, *"Machine-Assisted Grading of Nationwide School-Leaving Essay Exams with LLMs and Statistical NLP"* (arXiv:2601.16314), examines LLM-assisted grading of two full national cohorts of Estonian school-leaving essay exams, finding that in 60% of cases model scores fall within the range of human-rater disagreement. This is constrained to static essay grading; it does not address interactive, multi-turn technical interviews or adaptive difficulty, which PrepAIred's RL-driven difficulty adaptation is designed to add.
- **Mendonça et al. (2025)**, *"Evaluating LLMs for Automated Scoring in Formative Assessments"* (Applied Sciences, DOI: 10.3390/app15052787), reports a premium-LLM Spearman correlation of 0.963 against human programming-assessment scores and demonstrates practical equivalence to human grading within a defined threshold. This finding — that LLM scoring reliability is strong but domain- and prompt-dependent — directly informs PrepAIred's design choice to use an LLM (Qwen2.5) only as a *selective, secondary* adjustment mechanism rather than the primary scoring path, with the primary score computed deterministically (Sentence-BERT + FAISS + CrossEncoder).

## 3.3 Multi-Agent Coordination and Automated Scoring Decomposition

**Status: both citations confirmed real and accurately described.**

- **Wang et al. (2025)**, *"AutoSCORE: Enhancing Automated Scoring with Multi-Agent Large Language Models via Structured Component Recognition"* (arXiv:2509.21910), decomposes essay scoring across three specialist agents (Content, Structure, Language) coordinated by a "Chairman" agent applying rubric-aligned veto rules and score capping, outperforming single-agent LLM baselines on accuracy, quadratic-weighted kappa, and correlation metrics. **This is structurally close to PrepAIred's own evaluator design**: PrepAIred similarly decomposes scoring into distinct specialist components (S1 semantic similarity, S2 concept coverage, R reasoning quality) combined through a fixed weighted formula with deterministic post-hoc correction rules (mandatory-concept capping, mistake penalties) — AutoSCORE's chairman-agent veto/capping pattern is directly analogous to PrepAIred's `ScoreValidator` component.
- **Zhu et al. (2025)**, *"MultiAgentBench: Evaluating the Collaboration and Competition of LLM agents"* (ACL 2025, pp. 8580–8622), establishes a rigorous evaluation methodology for multi-agent task success, coordination, and planning across diverse interactive scenarios. It is not designed for interview scoring specifically, but its milestone-based evaluation framework for measuring coordination quality (not just task completion) is methodologically relevant to how this phase's own testing programme evaluates PrepAIred's orchestrator-and-guardrail coordination (Chapter 10, §10.3).

## 3.4 LLM Reliability, Reward Grounding, and Verification

**Status: mixed — one citation confirmed real but originally mischaracterized; one citation could not be verified at all.**

- **"Argos" (Microsoft Research)** is a real, published artifact — confirmed via live search (Microsoft Research blog and publication pages). However, **the original citation's description in `revised_report_v5.pdf` mischaracterizes its actual scope.** Argos is *"Agentic Reward for Grounded & Objective Scoring,"* a verification framework for **multimodal (visual/temporal) reinforcement learning** that grounds rewards in visual and temporal evidence to reduce hallucination in agentic RL training for tasks such as spatial reasoning and embodied AI/robotics benchmarks. It is **not**, as the original citation stated, specifically "a grounded reward verification framework... evaluated on instruction-following and scoring benchmarks" for LLM-based text evaluation pipelines. **Corrected framing:** Argos is relevant to this project only at the general-principle level — that grounding a reward/evaluation signal in verifiable evidence (rather than trusting an LLM's own judgment) reduces hallucination-driven errors — which is the same principle motivating PrepAIred's use of a deterministic FAISS/CrossEncoder pipeline as the *primary* evaluator, with the LLM (Qwen2.5) invoked only as a secondary, scope-limited adjustment.
- **"Auctores Online (2025)"**, originally cited for a multimodal signal-fusion-in-RL finding, **could not be located by live search under any plausible matching title, author, or description** (checked again, independently, 2026-11-13). "Auctores" is a known low-oversight/predatory open-access publishing platform name. **Removed from the final report (2026-11-13)** — not relocated or re-verified, so not reinstated. The underlying point it was used to support — that multimodal signal fusion improves reward/evaluation stability in RL — is adequately supported by the genuinely-verified Argos and MultiAgentBench findings above, so removing it does not weaken the argument.

## 3.5 Synthesis and Positioning

The verified literature establishes three findings relevant to PrepAIred: (1) decomposing automated scoring across specialist components with a coordinating validation layer (AutoSCORE) measurably outperforms monolithic LLM scoring — a pattern PrepAIred's own evaluator architecture follows; (2) LLM-based scoring is reliable but prompt/domain-dependent (Mendonça, Karjus), supporting PrepAIred's choice to keep the LLM as a secondary signal rather than the primary evaluator; (3) grounding evaluation/reward signals in verifiable evidence rather than trusting model judgment alone reduces hallucination risk (Argos, at the level of general principle), which is the same justification underlying PrepAIred's deterministic-primary/LLM-secondary evaluator design. No surveyed work combines rubric-anchored multi-component semantic evaluation, RL-driven difficulty adaptation under deterministic pedagogical guardrails, and prosodic audio signal integration within one interview-practice system — this remains PrepAIred's positioning, now stated with accurately verified supporting citations rather than the original, partially unverifiable set.
