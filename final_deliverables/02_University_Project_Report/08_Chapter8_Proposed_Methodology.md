# Chapter 8: Proposed Methodology

## 8.1 Evaluator Methodology and Rationale

Technical-interview answers are open-ended: two correct answers to the same question may share little surface vocabulary while being semantically and conceptually equivalent, so a single similarity metric is insufficient. PrepAIred decomposes evaluation into three mutually non-redundant signals:

- **S1 (semantic similarity, weight 0.15):** cosine similarity between the candidate's answer and a rubric model answer in SBERT embedding space. Weighted lowest because it is the least discriminating — two answers of very different quality can score similarly if they share vocabulary.
- **S2 (concept coverage, weight 0.35):** fraction of rubric-defined concept groups detected as present via FAISS nearest-neighbour search against a pre-computed concept-group index. The most discriminating component, because it directly measures whether specific expected ideas were addressed, and is interpretable (the exact covered/missing concepts are reportable to the candidate).
- **R (reasoning quality, weight 0.50):** a CrossEncoder jointly encoding the question and answer, sensitive to whether the answer actually responds to what was asked (as opposed to S1/S2's independent bi-encoder comparison). Weighted highest because logical coherence between question and answer is the most reliable discriminator of genuine understanding versus keyword-matching.

A keyword-stuffing defense dampens S2 by a factor of 0.60 whenever R ≤ 0.30 — preventing a candidate from scoring well by including relevant terminology without logically connecting it to the question asked. **This project's own canonical evidence record frames the resulting composite score as *safety-hardened rather than accuracy-optimal***: against a constructed human-rated benchmark, the full composite correlates with human judgment at ρ=0.38, below R-alone (ρ=0.48) or S1+R (ρ=0.49) — the S2 component's inclusion trades some raw correlation for interpretability and resistance to keyword-stuffing. This is a known, disclosed trade-off, not an unqualified accuracy claim.

**Correction verified directly against code in this phase (Chapter 10 §10.15):** this project's prior documentation described Qwen as being "invoked selectively for partial-credit adjustment." Direct inspection of `services/evaluator/app.py` (zero Qwen references anywhere in the file) and `agents/orchestrator/feedback_agent.py` (where `"final_score": round(score, 4)` is set from the deterministic `score` variable *before* the Qwen call, and is never reassigned from the Qwen response) confirms **Qwen never influences the numeric score under any code path found**. The composite score (S1/S2/R plus the Score Validator's deterministic corrections) is 100% deterministic. Qwen's real role, confirmed by code, is limited to: narrative feedback text generation (`what_candidate_said`, `how_to_answer`, `stronger_answer_guide`, `narrative_feedback`), contextual hint text, and follow-up question generation — none of which feed back into `final_score` or `grade`. This is a ninth confirmed report-vs-code discrepancy, and is also the central finding of the accompanying Qwen evaluator validation investigation (`testing_and_evaluation/Qwen_2.5_Evaluator_Validation_Report.md`).

## 8.2 Reinforcement Learning Methodology

The difficulty-adaptation problem is formulated as a discrete-time Markov Decision Process: at each turn the agent observes a 6-dimensional state (performance, rolling-average performance, audio confidence, hesitation, session progress, normalised difficulty), selects one of three actions (Easier/Same/Harder), and the episode terminates after a fixed question count.

**Training provenance (per this project's own canonical evidence record, not independently retrained or altered in this phase):** Proximal Policy Optimisation (Stable-Baselines3, `MlpPolicy`, [64,64] network), 24,576 timesteps per training seed (12 rollouts of 2,048 steps), training seeds {42, 123, 456, 789, 999}, evaluation seeds {1001, 2002, 3003, 4004, 5005}. The production checkpoint deployed in the live system is seed 123. **This corrects the Phase-2 report's claim of "500,000 steps" and seeds (42, 123, 777), which do not trace to any stored artifact** (Chapter 10 §10.2 documents this verification).

The reward function used in training combines decision alignment with a hand-coded rule oracle, outcome-delta reward, multimodal shaping, and a stability penalty. The project's own prior frozen research evidence (not reproduced here, cited as existing evidence) established that the trained PPO policy's raw contribution to tracking accuracy is largely attributable to the guardrail layer rather than the policy itself — i.e., "PPO improves tracking" is a claim the project's own evidence record has explicitly withdrawn in favour of the more precise finding that guardrails account for essentially all of the measured improvement over a fixed-difficulty baseline. This phase's own RL testing (Chapter 10 §10.3) is consistent with that finding: it found and fixed a genuine *guardrail* coverage gap, reinforcing that the guardrail layer — not the policy's own learned behaviour — is this architecture's primary safety mechanism.

## 8.3 Post-Inference Guardrail Methodology

Guardrails execute in a fixed, documented priority order (full conditions in Chapter 6 §6.2), designed around an explicit pedagogical priority: a candidate who is both struggling and anxious (G4) is the most vulnerable state and takes highest priority; escalating a strong candidate (G6) is the least urgent correction, since the cost of delayed escalation is lower than the cost of premature escalation or unaddressed anxiety. **This phase's testing (Chapter 10 §10.3) found this priority structure sound but found and closed a boundary gap** between the lowest-performance guardrails (G4/G1, triggering below 0.30) and the partial-understanding guardrail (G5, originally triggering only above 0.40) — a below-average-scoring candidate in the gap band could have had difficulty escalated by the raw policy with no safety check. The fix (widening G5's lower bound to 0.30) is documented with full rationale and verification in Chapter 10.

## 8.4 Audio Analysis Methodology

Four parallel sub-pipelines process each voice recording: transcription with forced word-level alignment (pause detection), prosodic feature extraction (pitch, energy, speaking rate via Librosa), linguistic analysis (filler-word and hedging-phrase detection), and an aggregation step combining these into two scalar signals (`confidence_score`, `hesitation_score` ∈ [0,1]) injected into both the RL state vector and the feedback pipeline. **Word-error-rate accuracy of the underlying transcription is not independently evaluated in this project**, since no verified reference-transcript dataset exists — this is stated as a methodological limitation, not resolved by this phase's testing (Chapter 10 confirms the pipeline runs correctly end-to-end but does not and cannot claim transcription accuracy).

## 8.5 Testing and Verification Methodology (this phase's own contribution)

Beyond the system's own operational methodology above, this phase applied a project-specific, evidence-graded testing methodology, detailed fully in Chapter 10: every category of testing was classified by actual relevance to this codebase (not generic coverage for its own sake); every reported number traces to a stored log, script, or git commit; genuine findings (the guardrail gap, the launcher crash, eight report-vs-code discrepancies) were diagnosed, where fixable, fixed with minimal scoped changes, and regression-verified; and unverifiable claims from prior documentation were neither repeated nor silently corrected without disclosure.
