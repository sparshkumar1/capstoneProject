ABSTRACT
Most existing technical interview preparation tools are static. Platforms such as LeetCode give every candidate the same sequence of questions regardless of how they are performing, offer no structured feedback, and do not capture any multimodal signal at all. PrepAIred is our attempt at closing that gap. At its core is a Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) agent, trained for 24,576 timesteps per seed across five seeds, that adjusts question difficulty based on how the candidate is doing. Answer quality is scored by a three-component semantic evaluator — Sentence-BERT similarity, FAISS-based concept coverage, and CrossEncoder reasoning-quality assessment — while a separate pipeline built on Whisper and Librosa pulls out prosodic signal such as confidence and hesitation from the candidate's speech. A locally hosted Qwen LLM generates hints and follow-up questions, and a dedicated FeedbackAgent turns every answer into a structured fifteen-field report covering misconception detection, concept gaps, and communication quality. All of this is coordinated by a single central InterviewOrchestrator; there is no multi-agent negotiation between separate components. Testing across three synthetic candidate profiles shows the system adapting difficulty correctly and degrading gracefully in all four critical failure scenarios we tried. Against human judgment, the composite evaluator reaches a Spearman correlation of ρ = 0.3812 — a moderate result that we report as exploratory rather than conclusive, since that is what the current evidence supports.

Chapter 1: Introduction
Technical interviews decide a lot about who gets hired in software engineering, and candidates are judged on more than whether they know the right algorithm or data structure. An interviewer is also watching whether someone can think out loud under pressure, explain a plan clearly, and respond when the question changes direction. Preparation tools have not really kept up with that. LeetCode, HackerRank, and InterviewBit all hand every candidate the same bank of questions at a handful of fixed difficulty levels, and none of them adjusts to how a specific candidate is actually performing. None gives structured feedback on a per-answer basis, and none pays attention to signals that only show up in how someone speaks — hesitation, confidence, how long they take to respond — even though these are clearly part of what a real interview is judging.
A fixed set of questions does not work well for anyone at the extremes: it is too easy for a strong candidate and discouraging for someone still building up the basics. Either way, what comes back is a pass/fail verdict or a single number, not anything that helps them improve. That gap between what a real interview actually involves and what existing preparation tools offer is what this project tries to close, and three things made that newly practical rather than just desirable. Adaptive learning approaches have consistently outperformed fixed-curriculum ones in the broader learning-systems literature. Transformer-based language models, vector similarity search, and open-source reinforcement-learning libraries are now mature enough that a system like this does not need an expensive proprietary API to run on. And per-answer feedback that covers reasoning quality, concept coverage, and specific misconceptions is simply more useful to a learner than one aggregate score — something none of the platforms surveyed in this project's literature review (Chapter 3) currently provide.
PrepAIred is our attempt at addressing these gaps directly. It brings together reinforcement-learning-based difficulty adaptation, a rubric-anchored semantic evaluator, prosodic audio analysis, and LLM-driven hints and feedback, with all of it coordinated by one central orchestrator rather than several agents negotiating with each other. This report describes it that way throughout: a single orchestrator coordinating specialised components, not a multi-agent system with arbitration between agents. The distinction matters here because a separate research-paper draft, supplied alongside this project's materials, describes a second "bounded-authority" coordination layer; checking the codebase directly showed that this layer does not correspond to anything actually implemented (Chapter 10, Section 10.13), so the architecture described in this report matches the real, verified system rather than that draft.
A Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) policy chooses between three actions — easier, same, or harder — based on a six-dimensional state vector encoding answer quality, rolling-average performance, audio confidence, hesitation, session progress, and current difficulty. If that choice would be a poor pedagogical call, a guardrail layer of six deterministic rules can override it afterward, and testing in this phase (Chapter 10) found that this guardrail layer, not the learned policy, is actually responsible for most of the system's measurable adaptive behaviour. Answer quality itself is scored by combining Sentence-BERT similarity, FAISS-based concept-coverage scoring, and CrossEncoder reasoning-quality assessment into one rubric-anchored composite score. A locally hosted Qwen2.5 model — confirmed by inspecting the code directly — handles the narrative side of things: feedback content, concept-level correctness and gap analysis (Chapter 6, Table 6.2), contextual hints, and follow-up questions. It plays no role in the numeric score itself, which is always set from a deterministic variable before Qwen is ever called. After every answer, a FeedbackAgent assembles a structured fifteen-field report covering misconception detection, concept-gap identification, and audio-derived communication patterns — a genuine step up from the bare scores that existing platforms hand back.

Chapter 2: Problem Definition
2.1 Problem Statement
Existing technical-interview preparation tools give every candidate a fixed sequence of questions at a fixed or self-selected difficulty. Where scoring exists at all, it's a binary pass/fail or a coarse numeric check, and none of it explains why an answer was scored as it was. Three specific, interrelated problems follow from that:
1. No individualised difficulty adaptation. A single fixed-difficulty question pool cuts both ways. It's too easy for advanced candidates, who get no useful challenge or signal from it. And it's too discouraging for candidates still building foundational competence, who get no scaffolded path to improvement.
2. No structured, actionable feedback. A numeric score alone doesn't tell a candidate which specific concept was missing, or which claim was factually wrong. It also says nothing about how their verbal delivery (hesitation, filler words, pacing) shaped the overall impression their answer made.
3. No multimodal signal capture. Existing tools evaluate only the textual or code content of an answer. Prosodic information (confidence, hesitation, pacing) gets discarded entirely, despite being a demonstrably relevant part of real interview performance.
2.2 Objectives
Functional objectives (as implemented and now verified in this phase; see Chapter 10 for the specific evidence behind each):
Select the next question's difficulty adaptively, using a reinforcement-learning policy constrained by deterministic pedagogical guardrails, verified against 13 constructed boundary cases (Chapter 10, Section 10.3).
Score free-form verbal answers through a rubric-anchored, multi-component semantic evaluator. This was verified against the project's existing human-rated benchmark (64 constructed answers, 8 questions, 3 raters) and against 10 newly constructed adversarial/edge-case inputs (Chapter 10, Section 10.4). Code answers take a completely separate path: isolated sandboxed execution against predefined test cases, not the semantic evaluator. This is detailed in Chapter 6, Section 6.2 vs. Chapter 8.
Capture prosodic audio signals (confidence, hesitation) and use them in both the adaptive-difficulty decision and the candidate-facing feedback.
Produce a structured, fifteen-field per-turn feedback report covering strengths, factual errors, missing concepts, and communication observations.
Execute and evaluate candidate-submitted C code inside an isolated sandbox. This was verified at the configuration level (Chapter 10, Section 10.6), and, with a live Docker daemon, at the execution level too, via the full 18-test Docker-dependent suite (Chapter 10, Section 10.18).
Persist all session and turn data to a relational store with verified referential integrity (Chapter 10, Section 10.5).
Non-functional objectives:
Graceful degradation: no single component's failure should crash a live session. This phase found and fixed one violation of that objective (the application launcher's Windows crash, Chapter 10, Section 10.1). It confirmed the objective otherwise holds for the evaluator, audio pipeline, and RL policy fallback paths.
Reasonable latency: this phase measured evaluator response time for the first time (median 896ms warm, Chapter 10, Section 10.7) as a baseline for future optimisation. This figure is distinct from, and not claimed to reproduce, the project's earlier Paper-1 latency measurement, which was taken under different hardware and conditions.
A scoped, documented security posture for the one component that executes untrusted input (candidate-submitted code), verified at the configuration level and, in this phase's later extension, at the execution level too (Chapter 10, Section 10.18).
2.3 Scope
In scope: adaptive technical interviews over spoken/typed verbal answers and C code submissions, covering Data Structures & Algorithms and C-language topics (the current 125-question bank), single-candidate sessions, and the testing/verification programme documented in Chapter 10.
Explicitly out of scope (stated here to prevent scope creep in later chapters, in keeping with the discipline applied throughout this phase): video or facial/behavioural analysis, which isn't implemented. Also out of scope is a multi-agent "bounded-authority coordination" layer described in an auxiliary research-paper draft; on inspection, it was confirmed not to exist in the codebase (see Chapter 10, Section 10.13). New PPO training, reward redesign, or RL experiment campaigns are out too, governed by the project's existing experiment-preregistration requirements and not undertaken in this phase. And formal human-subject usability or learning-outcome evaluation is left as future work.
2.4 Assumptions and Constraints
The system assumes a single candidate per session and English-language spoken or typed input. It also assumes a locally available compute environment able to run SBERT, FAISS, a fine-tuned CrossEncoder, and a quantised 1.5B-parameter local LLM.
Code evaluation needs a Docker daemon on the host. That daemon wasn't actually running when this phase's testing started, so the resulting gap in test coverage got written up and fully attributed rather than papered over (Chapter 10, Section 10.9). It didn't stay unresolved, either — once a reachable daemon came online, a later, separately-scoped extension went back and closed it (Chapter 10, Section 10.18).
We haven't independently verified audio-pipeline accuracy in terms of word-error-rate, mainly because there is no verified reference-transcript dataset to measure it against. That's a limit on what this report can claim, and it stays open rather than being resolved here.

Chapter 3: Literature Review
Building this project meant reading across a few different strands of work: automated interview evaluation, LLM-based scoring, how reliable (or not) LLM judgments actually are, how multi-agent coordination gets benchmarked, and how multimodal signals get fused in reinforcement learning. The Phase-2 report (Ch.2) had already assembled six citations for this survey, but we didn't take any of them on faith — each one went through an independent, live re-verification pass in this phase, on the principle that a reference earns its place by tracing to a real, checkable source, not by having appeared in an earlier draft. What follows reports that verification status openly, including where it went wrong: that is part of this phase's evidence discipline, not a cosmetic citation list.
3.1 Automated Interview Evaluation Systems
Status: resolved. The originally cited "Wu et al. (2026)" description names an AI-driven interview evaluation system coordinating multiple agents across voice, facial expression, and text signals via virtual digital-human avatars. It couldn't be matched to a specific, exactly-corresponding paper via live search, either in this phase or on a fresh independent re-check. We replace it with CoMAI [1], Sun, Yu, Yin, Yang, Zhang, and Xu's "CoMAI: A Collaborative Multi-Agent Framework for Robust and Equitable Interview Evaluation" (arXiv:2603.16215, 2026). It is a verified, closely-related real paper describing a multi-agent interview-evaluation system coordinated by a centralized finite-state machine. It is independently confirmed to report 90.47% assessment accuracy (a 30.47-point improvement over single-agent baselines and 19.05 points over human interviewers), 83.33% recall, and a 100% defense success rate against 500+ adversarial attacks in its own evaluation. PrepAIred differs from this line of work in a basic way. It evaluates the substantive correctness of an answer's content through a rubric-anchored semantic pipeline, not primarily behavioural or delivery signals. And it is a single-controller system, not a multi-agent one.
3.2 LLM-Based Automated Scoring and Grading
Status: both citations confirmed real and accurately described.
Karjus et al. (2026) [2], "Machine-Assisted Grading of Nationwide School-Leaving Essay Exams with LLMs and Statistical NLP" (arXiv:2601.16314), examines LLM-assisted grading across two full national cohorts of Estonian school-leaving essay exams. It finds that model scores fall within the range of human-rater disagreement in 60% of cases. The work stays within static essay grading, though. It doesn't touch interactive, multi-turn technical interviews or adaptive difficulty, which is exactly what PrepAIred's RL-driven difficulty adaptation is designed to add.
Mendonça et al. (2025) [3], "Evaluating LLMs for Automated Scoring in Formative Assessments" (Applied Sciences, DOI: 10.3390/app15052787), reports a premium-LLM Spearman correlation of 0.963 against human programming-assessment scores. It also shows practical equivalence to human grading within a defined threshold. The underlying finding, that LLM scoring reliability is strong but domain- and prompt-dependent, directly informs PrepAIred's design choice. An LLM (Qwen2.5) is used only as a selective, secondary adjunct, never the primary scoring path. The primary score itself is computed deterministically (Sentence-BERT + FAISS + CrossEncoder).
3.3 Multi-Agent Coordination and Automated Scoring Decomposition
Status: both citations confirmed real and accurately described.
Wang et al. (2025) [4], "AutoSCORE: Enhancing Automated Scoring with Multi-Agent Large Language Models via Structured Component Recognition" (arXiv:2509.21910), decomposes essay scoring across three specialist agents (Content, Structure, Language) coordinated by a "Chairman" agent that applies rubric-aligned veto rules and score capping. It outperforms single-agent LLM baselines on accuracy, quadratic-weighted kappa, and correlation metrics. This sits structurally close to PrepAIred's own evaluator design. PrepAIred likewise decomposes scoring into distinct specialist components (S1 semantic similarity, S2 concept coverage, R reasoning quality), combined through a fixed weighted formula with deterministic post-hoc correction rules (mandatory-concept capping, mistake penalties). AutoSCORE's chairman-agent veto/capping pattern maps directly onto PrepAIred's ScoreValidator component. Neither system's decomposition implies autonomous, negotiating agents; both instead use specialised, deterministically-coordinated components.
Zhu et al. (2025) [5], "MultiAgentBench: Evaluating the Collaboration and Competition of LLM agents" (ACL 2025, pp. 8580–8622), sets out a rigorous evaluation methodology for multi-agent task success, coordination, and planning across diverse interactive scenarios. It wasn't built for interview scoring. But its milestone-based evaluation framework, which measures coordination quality and not just task completion, is methodologically relevant to how this phase's own testing programme evaluates PrepAIred's orchestrator-and-guardrail coordination (Chapter 10, Section 10.3).
3.4 LLM Reliability, Reward Grounding, and Verification
Status: mixed. One citation is confirmed real but was originally mischaracterized; one citation could not be verified at all.
"Argos" (Microsoft Research) [6] is a real, published artifact, confirmed via live search (Microsoft Research blog and publication pages). That said, the original citation's description in the Phase-2 report mischaracterizes its actual scope. Argos is "Agentic Reward for Grounded & Objective Scoring," a verification framework for multimodal (visual/temporal) reinforcement learning. It grounds rewards in visual and temporal evidence to reduce hallucination in agentic RL training, aimed at tasks such as spatial reasoning and embodied AI/robotics benchmarks. It is not, as the original citation claimed, specifically "a grounded reward verification framework... evaluated on instruction-following and scoring benchmarks" for LLM-based text evaluation pipelines. Corrected framing: Argos matters to this project only at the level of a general principle. Grounding a reward or evaluation signal in verifiable evidence, instead of trusting an LLM's own judgment, reduces hallucination-driven errors. That is the same principle behind PrepAIred's use of a deterministic FAISS/CrossEncoder pipeline as the sole source of the numeric score. The LLM (Qwen2.5) is invoked only for a secondary, scope-limited role generating feedback content (narrative text, hints, follow-up questions). It never reads, adjusts, or otherwise influences the score itself (confirmed by direct code inspection, Chapter 6, Table 6.2; Chapter 10, Section 10.15).
"Auctores Online (2025)", originally cited for a multimodal signal-fusion-in-RL finding, couldn't be located by live search under any plausible matching title, author, or description. "Auctores" is a known low-oversight, predatory open-access publishing platform name, so we remove it from the final report instead of relocating or re-verifying it. The point it originally supported (that multimodal signal fusion improves reward/evaluation stability in RL) is adequately covered by the genuinely-verified Argos and MultiAgentBench findings above. So removing it doesn't weaken the argument.
3.5 Foundational Background: Adaptive Testing, Tutoring, and Interview-Performance Prediction
Beyond the six interview- and scoring-specific citations verified above, two other core design choices behind PrepAIred draw on older, well-established literature outside the LLM-era scoring work surveyed in 3.1–3.4. Those are adapting difficulty to the candidate, and treating the interview as a multimodal signal. Adaptive difficulty selection has a long history in psychometrics, going back to computerized adaptive testing [7]. PrepAIred's PPO-driven difficulty controller extends that tradition with a reward-learned policy, not a purely psychometric one. Intelligent tutoring systems modelled learner mastery through cognitive tutors [8] and knowledge tracing [9]. PrepAIred's rolling per-candidate performance state (Chapter 8) plays an analogous role in a narrower, interview-specific setting. Curriculum learning in reinforcement learning [10], [11] motivates the general principle of adjusting task difficulty progressively to a learner's or agent's current competence. PrepAIred's guardrail-constrained PPO policy applies that principle at the level of individual interview questions, not a training curriculum. Separately, automated prediction of interview performance from multimodal (audio/visual/text) features [12], [13] establishes that non-verbal and prosodic signals carry genuine predictive value in interview assessment. That finding motivates PrepAIred's own prosodic confidence/hesitation signal capture (Chapter 6), albeit through a narrower, audio-only slice of it.
3.6 Synthesis and Positioning
Three findings from this verified literature matter most to PrepAIred. Decomposing automated scoring across specialist components under a coordinating validation layer (AutoSCORE [4]) measurably outperforms monolithic LLM scoring. PrepAIred's own evaluator architecture follows that pattern. LLM-based scoring is reliable but prompt- and domain-dependent (Mendonça [3], Karjus [2]). That supports PrepAIred's choice to keep the LLM as a secondary signal instead of the primary evaluator. And grounding evaluation and reward signals in verifiable evidence, instead of trusting model judgment alone, reduces hallucination risk (Argos [6], at the level of general principle). That is the same justification behind PrepAIred's deterministic-primary, LLM-secondary evaluator design. No surveyed work combines rubric-anchored multi-component semantic evaluation, RL-driven difficulty adaptation under deterministic pedagogical guardrails, and prosodic audio signal integration within one interview-practice system. That remains PrepAIred's positioning, now backed by accurately verified citations instead of the original, partially unverifiable set.

Chapter 5: High-Level Design (HLD)
5.1 Architectural Overview
No single monolithic component in PrepAIred is responsible for the end-to-end interview process. The system is instead decomposed into specialised components, each with a clearly bounded responsibility, all coordinated by a central orchestrator. This gives the architecture separation of concerns, fault isolation, and the ability to degrade gracefully when an individual component is unavailable.
This is a single-controller, hub-and-spoke architecture, not a multi-agent system with inter-component negotiation or arbitration. A single authoritative orchestrator owns all session state, and every other component is a stateless or independently-stateful service that the orchestrator invokes. None of them talk to each other directly. There is one documented exception, and it is narrow: the FeedbackAgent issues its own request to the Qwen service instead of being routed through the orchestrator. That is an implementation detail, not peer-to-peer negotiation. We say this plainly because a separate, auxiliary research-paper draft describing a second, "bounded-authority" coordination layer was supplied alongside this project's materials. Direct inspection of the codebase in this phase confirmed that layer does not exist in the implementation (Chapter 10, Section 10.13). This report's architecture description is written to match the real, verified system instead.
The system spans four logical layers here, corrected against the five the Phase-2 report described. The "Input Analysis Layer" and "Orchestration Layer" distinction from that report is retained below. One claim does need correction, though: that question selection belongs to an "Evaluation Layer" alongside FAISS-based retrieval. In fact, question selection has no FAISS or embedding dependency at all (Section 5.1, item 4, and Chapter 10 Section 10.8):
1. Candidate Layer. Everything starts in the browser, with the candidate and the Interview UI (apps/web/). A candidate can answer two ways — speaking into the browser's MediaRecorder API, or writing code in a CodeMirror-powered editor — and both travel to the backend over one persistent WebSocket connection, which also carries everything coming back the other way: the current question, the difficulty indicator, the hint panel, real-time feedback. We kept this layer deliberately thin on purpose. No business logic lives in the frontend, which means the interview logic underneath can be tested on its own, without needing the UI at all.
2. Input Analysis Layer. A raw candidate response isn't usable yet — it has to become structured signals before evaluation or strategy can touch it. Three sub-pipelines run in parallel to get there: speech-to-text with forced word-level alignment (OpenAI Whisper / faster-whisper), prosodic feature extraction (Librosa), and a session timer that turns elapsed time into a normalised pace feature. The orchestrator only sees the response once these three come back together as one multimodal package. Chapter 8, Section 8.4 has the full design.
3. Orchestration Layer. agents/orchestrator/interview_orchestrator.py is the sole authoritative writer of session state. It is instantiated once per session and exposes a clean public API that the backend's WebSocket dispatcher delegates all incoming events to. An asyncio.Lock serialises every mutating operation to prevent race conditions, and each incoming event (voice answer, code submission, hint request, or session termination) is routed to the appropriate downstream component. It is the only component with visibility across the rest of the system; no other component talks to another directly.
4. Evaluation and Strategy Layer. This layer covers the semantic evaluator (services/evaluator/app.py), the coding sandbox (agents/coding_executor/), the RL/guardrail strategy layer (agents/strategy/hybrid_orchestrator.py + rl/guardrails.py), the FeedbackAgent, and the question selector. The evaluator is verified to be a Flask application, not FastAPI as the Phase-2 report stated (Chapter 10, Section 10.4). For verbal answers, the evaluator runs a three-component pipeline (Similarity, Concept Coverage, Reasoning) and combines the outputs into a final weighted score. That score then passes through a deterministic Score Validator for post-hoc correction. Code submissions take a different path: the coding executor runs submitted code in a sandboxed environment against predefined test cases and analyses it for algorithmic complexity and structural correctness. Both paths return a canonical score to the orchestrator through the same scoring interface. They remain two genuinely separate code paths, though: direct inspection confirmed zero cross-imports between them, consistent with the project's own stated design. The question selector does not use FAISS or any semantic-retrieval mechanism, correcting the Phase-2 report's architecture diagrams. The production path, apps.backend.main.select_questions, is a fully deterministic scored-candidate selection (difficulty distance + topic-diversity penalty + personalisation adjustment, with three-level deduplication, no randomness). Chapter 6, Section 6.6 corrects the attribution against an earlier draft of this chapter. That draft described a separate baseline/research module (agents/question_selector/question_selector.py, which does contain random.choice tie-breaking) as if it were the production design. Neither module depends on FAISS or embeddings; the FAISS index that does exist in the project is used exclusively inside the evaluator, for concept-coverage scoring of answers, not for selecting questions.

5.3 Design Principles
Three principles guided every architectural decision in PrepAIred. Separation of concerns comes first: every component has a single, clearly bounded responsibility, so each one can be tested and replaced independently without touching the others. This is exactly what let this phase's dependency-injection-based test suite (Chapter 9, Section 9.1) exercise every logical branch of the orchestrator without starting any external service. Graceful degradation comes second: every layer implements a fallback so that one component failing does not disrupt the candidate's session. This phase's testing (Chapter 10) confirmed the principle holds for the evaluator, audio pipeline, and RL policy fallback paths. It also found and fixed the one place where it did not: the application launcher itself. Orchestrator sovereignty is the third: all domain logic lives inside the orchestrator. That keeps the backend's WebSocket dispatcher a thin router with no knowledge of interview semantics, verified directly by inspecting apps/backend/main.py (Chapter 7, Section 7.1). Chapters 6, 8, and 9 present the detailed design and implementation of each component.
5.4 Session Lifecycle
A session moves through five states (created → in_progress → complete | abandoned | error), and these are exposed only as public methods on the orchestrator, never as direct state mutation from the dispatcher layer. Session termination is idempotent: the first end() call generates and caches the full report, and every later call just returns that cached version without re-running any generation logic. This keeps things consistent under the concurrent disconnect-and-end-session events that are common on mobile connections.
5.5 Adaptive Difficulty Pipeline (high-level)
1. RL sits out the first one or two questions entirely. Question 1 is pinned at difficulty 2 (easy) and question 2 at difficulty 3 (mid) by a fixed schedule, no policy involved. If those two scores disagree sharply enough that the candidate's real ability level is still unclear, a third baseline question runs before RL is allowed to take over.
2. The RL policy doesn't start from a blank slate — its initial difficulty is seeded from the average of those baseline scores, so the first real RL decision already has a reasonably well-informed state vector to work from rather than an uninformed prior.
3. The six-dimensional state vector draws on the latest answer score, the rolling average, audio confidence/hesitation, session progress, and current difficulty.
4. The frozen PPO policy (rl/checkpoints/seed_123/) then proposes a raw action from {Easier, Same, Harder}.
5. Six deterministic guardrail rules apply in strict priority order, and the first one whose condition is met overrides the raw action. This phase found and closed a real gap in this layer in the canonical module, rl/guardrails.py; see Chapter 10, Section 10.3. The live orchestrator evaluates its own separate, hand-duplicated copy of these rules, not `rl/guardrails.py` directly. That copy has three confirmed, disclosed divergences from the canonical fix (Chapter 6, Section 6.3; Chapter 10, Section 10.3; Chapter 11, Section 11.4).
6. The resulting action updates difficulty (clamped to [1,5]) for the next question.
Chapter 6 (LLD) and Chapter 8 (Methodology) give the full detail: state-vector construction, guardrail conditions and rationale, and the reward function as used in the project's prior frozen RL research.

This chapter goes down to code-level detail for the five pieces that matter most to what this project actually contributes: the orchestrator, the evaluator, the RL/guardrail strategy layer, the FeedbackAgent, and the database schema. 6.1 Interview Orchestrator (`agents/orchestrator/interview_orchestrator.py`)
If there's one module that counts as this project's centrepiece, it's this one — at roughly 2,300 lines of Python (wc -l, re-counted in this correction pass; an earlier draft of this chapter had understated it as "approximately 600 lines," which is corrected here). Baseline-phase management, evaluator invocation, RL policy consultation, feedback generation, question selection, report assembly — all of it lives inside this single class, and nothing outside it is allowed to make a domain decision. That concentration of responsibility isn't free, though: a WebSocket-driven async backend can deliver duplicate or out-of-order events, the class still has to be testable in isolation without spinning up every dependent service, its session state has to stay simultaneously readable by REST endpoints with backward-compatible contracts while also being mutated by async WebSocket handlers, and termination itself has to behave safely even when disconnect and end-session signals arrive at almost the same moment. Four implementation decisions, described below, are what actually make that work:
Concurrency safety via `asyncio.Lock`. One orchestrator instance lives for exactly one WebSocket session, and because an async FastAPI handler is what's touching it, events can and do arrive out of order or duplicated — picture a client retransmitting a voice_answer after a network hiccup, which lands two evaluations of the same answer at once. Without a guard, that scenario mutates session state from two coroutines simultaneously, and the damage shows up as inconsistent scores, a wrong performance history, a difficulty trajectory that no longer makes sense. The fix is a per-instance asyncio.Lock, taken at the start of every public mutating method and released on the way out. Scoping it to the instance rather than the module matters: two sessions running at once in the same process never block each other, they're fully independent, while inside any one session everything is still serialised one event at a time with no cross-session cost. A thread lock was never really in the running here — FastAPI's async handlers all share one single-threaded event loop, so a thread lock would add overhead for no benefit; it wouldn't even stop interleaving within that loop.
Dependency injection for testability. Rather than importing the evaluator function and the question-selection function directly, the orchestrator takes both as constructor arguments — its two most complex dependencies, handed in rather than reached for. That one choice is what makes full test isolation possible: swap in a deterministic mock evaluator that always hands back a fixed score tuple, or a mock selector that always returns the same question, and nothing needs the FAISS index loaded, Sentence-BERT weights pulled in, or the Qwen microservice running. Every logical branch — baseline-phase transitions, guardrail activations, follow-up injections, session termination — ends up testable in milliseconds with zero external dependencies, which is exactly what this phase's own 25-file automated test suite (Chapter 10) leans on.
State management and REST compatibility. One dictionary holds all mutable session state and serves as the single source of truth for it. When a REST endpoint needs to read that state, it gets a shallow copy back through a dedicated accessor, never the dictionary itself. Things that are really just internal bookkeeping — the question queue, the current question index, attempt counts, the timer snapshot, the cached report — live as plain instance variables instead, which is why the state dictionary stays clean and serialisable while those operational details stay out of view.
Idempotent session termination. Calling the session-termination method the first time generates the full report, caches it, and hands it back; every call after that just returns what's already cached, no regeneration. That matters because a WebSocket disconnect and an explicit end-session message from the client have a habit of arriving almost together, especially on mobile connections, and without idempotency those two concurrent termination calls could each spin up their own report generation — producing two different reports, or a race in which one overwrites the cache the other just wrote. Pairing the lock with a cache check closes that off completely.
Adaptive-difficulty pipeline. There's a single private method that runs after every answer is scored and carries the whole baseline-plus-RL-plus-guardrail decision inside it. The first thing it checks is whether the current question index is still inside the baseline phase. If so, it applies the deterministic baseline logic and returns without consulting the RL policy. Otherwise it builds the six-dimensional observation vector from session state and applies the stored normalisation statistics. Next it calls the strategy layer's suggest() method for the raw policy action, then passes that action through the guardrail pipeline (priority order G4 → G1 → G2 → G5 → G6). The resolved action executes last, and it always updates difficulty. ("G3" does not name a separate rule; it is an alternate label the canonical module's own docstring uses for G5; see Section 6.3.) Hint and follow-up generation are a separate, independently-triggered behaviour, not an alternative outcome of this same action-resolution step. Chapter 8 draws the precise distinction between the PPO policy's three-action output and these guardrail-triggered behaviours. Keeping this pipeline centralised in one method means any change to the adaptation logic (a new guardrail, a change to the reward-relevant state, a different RL policy) only needs to happen in one place. This method is the live orchestrator's own guardrail implementation, not a call into the canonical `rl.guardrails.apply_canonical_guardrails()` function documented in Section 6.3. The two have diverged. See Section 6.3's disambiguation note and Chapter 10, Section 10.3.
Follow-up question injection. When the resolved action calls for a follow-up, the orchestrator sends a request to the Qwen microservice (port 8001), with the current question and the candidate's most recent answer as context. The microservice returns a contextually coherent follow-up question, which gets inserted into the question queue immediately after the current position. The client never notices the difference: the next "advance to next question" call just retrieves the next item in the queue. That item happens to be the injected follow-up, treated identically to any other question. No change to the WebSocket protocol or the frontend was needed to support this.

6.3 RL / Guardrail Strategy Layer
Class: agents.strategy.hybrid_orchestrator.HybridOrchestrator
__init__(model_path="rl/checkpoints/seed_123/ppo_final.zip",
          vec_path="rl/checkpoints/seed_123/vecnormalize.pkl")

       or observation_space != Box(6,)); sets self.ready / self.is_compatible
 
suggest(score, current_difficulty, session) -> (new_difficulty, reason, action_name)
    1. if baseline phase: return the fixed baseline schedule, RL not consulted
    2. build 6D observation: build_rl_observation(score, current_difficulty, session)
    3. normalize: (obs - obs_mean) / sqrt(obs_var + 1e-8), clipped to [-10, 10]
    4. if PPO ready: action_idx = model.predict(norm_obs, deterministic=True)
       else: action_idx = heuristic fallback (score >= 0.75 -> Harder,
                                               score <  0.40 -> Easier, else Same)
    5. clamp resulting difficulty to [1, 5]

That deterministic=True flag isn't a default left over from somewhere — it's doing real work. Training wants the policy sampling stochastically from its action probabilities, since that's how exploration happens, but carrying that same stochasticity into inference would mean the same candidate state could produce a different decision on different runs. Setting deterministic=True instead makes the policy always take the highest-probability (greedy) action, so the same input gives the same output every time, which is what a deployed system actually needs. Separately, the VecNormalize statistics (obs_rms.mean, obs_rms.var, saved in vecnormalize.pkl) don't apply themselves — they have to be applied by hand at inference time, because VecNormalize wraps the Gym environment during training rather than living inside the model. Skip that step and the policy network, whose weights were calibrated against normalised inputs, tends to degrade quietly — a known failure mode for deployed PPO systems generally.
6-D observation vector (build_rl_observation): [perf, avg_perf (last-5 rolling mean), conf, hes, progress (t/T), diff_norm (diff/5)], each clamped to [0,1] with NaN/Inf guarded to a safe default (0.5 for confidence when unmeasured, 1 - conf for hesitation when unmeasured).
Canonical vs. live implementation: read before the rule list below. This guardrail logic actually exists twice in the codebase, and the two copies disagree. One is the canonical module, rl.guardrails.apply_canonical_guardrails(perf, avg_perf, conf, hes, progress, difficulty, proposed_action, consecutive_failures=0, is_infrastructure_failure=False) -> (final_action, was_overridden, guardrail_id) — documented below, called by the RL training/simulation environment (rl/env/interview_env.py), and the thing this phase's own boundary tests (Chapter 10, Section 10.3) exercise directly. The other is what actually runs in production: agents/orchestrator/interview_orchestrator.py::_apply_guardrails, invoked from _adapt_difficulty. It isn't a call into the canonical function at all — it's a separately hand-written copy that was never kept in sync with it. Checking the two against each other in this correction pass turned up three confirmed places where they disagree:
G5 boundary: canonical uses 0.30 <= perf < 0.65 (the widened, fixed boundary below); the live copy still uses the original, unfixed 0.40 < perf < 0.65; already disclosed in Chapter 10, Section 10.3 and Chapter 11, Section 11.4.
G6 "gap" definition: canonical computes gap = perf - diff_norm (performance relative to the current question's difficulty); the live copy computes gap = perf - avg_perf (performance relative to the candidate's own rolling average): a substantively different signal, not a renamed variable.
G6 "nervous_expert" definition: canonical is (0.80 < perf < 0.95 AND hes > 0.65) OR (conf < 0.40 AND hes > 0.60); the live copy implements only the second disjunct, so a high-performing-but-hesitant candidate (the first disjunct's case) is not protected from escalation in the live path the way the canonical module's own documented rationale (below) describes.
This report doesn't quietly fix any of those three in the live copy — they stay as open, disclosed findings instead (Chapter 10, Section 10.3; Chapter 11, Section 11.4), consistent with this project's rule against silently patching something once it's been flagged. The rule list, priority order, and rationale that follow all describe the canonical module:
1. Infrastructure-failure override → Same (always). If a turn failed for an infrastructure reason rather than a genuine answer-quality one, difficulty degradation simply doesn't apply.
2. G4 (perf < 0.30 AND hes > 0.60) → Easier. This one goes first because a candidate who is both struggling and anxious is in the most vulnerable state the system can see, and that calls for immediate support.
3. G1 (perf < 0.30 AND 0.4 ≤ diff_norm ≤ 0.7) → Easier. Catches a candidate who keeps underperforming at a moderate difficulty and backs off the load.
4. G2 (conf < 0.30 AND hes > 0.70 AND perf < 0.80) → Same (or Easier if hesitation exceeds 0.85). The point of this rule is telling a nervous expert apart from someone who's genuinely struggling, so a confidence dip alone doesn't get penalised as if it were a knowledge gap.
5. G5 (0.30 ≤ perf < 0.65 AND avg_perf < 0.60 AND consecutive_failures < 2) → Same. Mid-range performance combined with a below-average trend over the session is read as a reason to hold difficulty steady rather than push it up. This phase widened the lower bound from 0.40 to 0.30, closing a verified coverage gap (Chapter 10, Section 10.3) — before that fix, a candidate scoring in [0.30, 0.40) with moderate confidence/hesitation had no guardrail watching them at all, and the raw policy was free to escalate their difficulty with nothing to stop it.
6. G6 (perf ≥ 0.90 AND gap > 0.25 AND NOT nervous_expert) → Harder. This sits last because escalating a strong candidate is the least time-sensitive of these corrections — waiting one extra turn to escalate costs less than escalating too early or missing real anxiety would. The nervous_expert flag (canonical definition: (0.80 < perf < 0.95 AND hes > 0.65) OR (conf < 0.40 AND hes > 0.60); see this section's divergence note above) holds this escalation back for candidates whose audio signals read as anxious despite strong content — on the theory that their hesitation is about delivery nerves, not an actual knowledge gap.
7. Else: nothing fires, and the raw proposed action goes through unchanged.

6.4 FeedbackAgent: Fifteen-Field Structured Feedback
Every evaluated turn triggers the FeedbackAgent, and what comes out the other end is a fifteen-field feedback dictionary — readable by the session logger as data, and readable by a human in the frontend report view. Under the hood it works in four stages.
Score decomposition and grading. The report opens by taking the score apart: the final weighted number, the letter grade that range maps to, and the S1/S2/R components individually alongside how much each one actually contributed. A candidate looking at this can tell at a glance which dimension of their answer dragged the score down.
Misconception detection, corrected in this correction pass. feedback_agent.py has its own topic-keyed dictionary of trigger phrases mapped to corrections (_detect_incorrect()), plus two more helpers, _strong_points() and _improvement_tips(). Checking the file directly in this correction pass turned up something unexpected: none of those three methods is ever actually called. They're dead code, sitting there but never wired into generate()'s real execution path. What the live incorrect_or_incomplete, strong_points, and missing_concepts fields actually draw on is the evaluator's own eval_result output (covered_concepts, missing_concepts, incorrect_claims) — numbers that the evaluator's FAISS concept-matching and substring misconception detection already produced back in Section 6.2, not anything these FeedbackAgent helpers touch. The underlying technique doesn't change — it's still substring matching either way, just running inside the evaluator rather than inside FeedbackAgent — so the precision/recall trade-off below still applies; only the attribution changes. That makes this a dead-code finding at the FeedbackAgent level, not a finding about how misconception detection itself works, and it's logged as a newly-found limitation in Chapter 11. On the trade-off itself: substring matching over embedding-based similarity is a deliberate choice, because a correction firing only when the candidate demonstrably said something wrong is worth more here than firing whenever an answer merely resembles a known misconception. That buys precision at the cost of recall, and Chapter 11 states that trade-off as an acknowledged limitation rather than hiding it.
Communication analysis. Four signals feed this stage — filler-word count, hesitation rate (the audio pipeline's hesitation_score), how often hedging language shows up, and how long the response runs — each one checked against its own threshold to produce a targeted delivery tip. When the audio pipeline isn't available and confidence/hesitation fall back to a neutral 0.5, communication_tips comes back as an empty list rather than a guess dressed up as an assessment. That's the right call: saying nothing about delivery beats reporting delivery metrics that were never actually measured.
Content analysis and trend tracking. Four fields make up the content side — strong_points, missing_concepts, incorrect_or_incomplete, and vague_points (things the candidate gestured at without developing enough to count as covered) — which together read as a revision checklist tied directly to the rubric that produced the score. Trend tracking runs alongside this: the current turn's score gets compared against the rolling mean of the last three, and a swing of ±0.08 or more is reported as "Improving" or "Declining," while anything smaller stays "Stable." That threshold exists specifically so ordinary score noise doesn't get reported as a trend that isn't really there.

7.2 Concurrency and State-Integrity Design
The orchestrator is the sole writer of authoritative session state, guarded by a per-instance asyncio.Lock acquired at the entry of every public mutating method. This guards specifically against a real hazard in a WebSocket-driven async backend: a client retransmitting an event after a network hiccup could otherwise deliver two concurrent evaluations of the same answer. That could corrupt the rolling-average performance history or the difficulty trajectory. Session termination is idempotent through a cached-report check inside that same lock, which guards against the WebSocket-disconnect-and-explicit-end-session race that is common on mobile connections.

Chapter 8: Proposed Methodology
8.1 Evaluator Methodology and Rationale
The Evaluator's job is to turn a candidate's free-form verbal answer into a structured numerical assessment. That is harder than it sounds, because technical interview answers are open-ended: two correct answers to the same question can share almost no surface-level vocabulary and still be semantically and conceptually equivalent. No single similarity metric captures that well on its own. That is why PrepAIred splits evaluation into three mutually non-redundant components, each sensitive to a different dimension of answer quality. A fixed weighted formula then combines them.
S1: Semantic Similarity (weight 0.15). S1 is the cosine similarity between the sentence embedding of the candidate's transcript and the sentence embedding of the rubric's model answer. Both are encoded with the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 Sentence-BERT model [14] (384-dimensional dense vectors, trained on natural-language-inference and semantic-textual-similarity tasks). It gets the lowest weight of the three because it is also the least discriminating. An answer that correctly identifies the key concepts and an answer that merely drops in relevant terminology without demonstrating any understanding can end up with similar S1 scores. They just need to share enough vocabulary in the embedding space. In practice S1 works best as a coarse filter, separating completely off-topic responses from substantive ones, instead of as a fine-grained quality signal.
S2: Concept Coverage (weight 0.35). S2 is the most discriminating of the two deterministic similarity signals, which is why it carries the higher of their two weights. It measures the fraction of rubric-defined concept groups the candidate's response actually addresses. Each question's rubric defines a set of these groups. A dynamic-programming question, for instance, might have concept groups for optimal substructure, overlapping subproblems, memoisation, and bottom-up tabulation. Each group is encoded offline with the same Sentence-BERT model and stored in a FAISS [15] flat index alongside its metadata. At inference time the candidate's transcript is encoded and used to query that index. A concept group counts as covered once the maximum cosine similarity between the transcript and the group's embedding clears a fixed threshold. The advantage over a purely embedding-based approach is that this stays rubric-anchored and interpretable. The exact set of covered and missing concept groups can be reported straight back to the candidate as actionable feedback. That kind of traceability is one of PrepAIred's core design requirements.
R: Reasoning Quality (weight 0.50). R comes from a CrossEncoder model, a fine-tuned ms-marco-MiniLM derivative. It jointly encodes the question and the candidate's answer through a shared transformer attention mechanism, instead of encoding them separately and comparing afterward the way S1 and S2's bi-encoder approach does. Because it attends to both at once, it produces a relevance score that is sensitive to the logical relationship between question and answer, not just their vocabulary overlap. That makes R especially good at catching answers that are factually correct but miss the actual question. A candidate asked about time complexity who instead correctly describes space complexity will still score moderately on S1 and S2. R, though, will register the mismatch with a low score. R gets the highest weight of the three precisely because logical coherence between question and answer is the most reliable way to tell genuine understanding apart from keyword-matching.
Combination and keyword-stuffing defense. To stop a candidate from scoring well just by dropping in relevant terminology without logically connecting it to the question, S2 is dampened by a factor of 0.60 whenever R ≤ 0.30. Equation (1) then gives the final score, plus rubric-driven bonus/penalty terms, clamped to [0,1]. A further cap applies on top of that, regardless of what the weighted formula alone would produce, if the answer fails to address a mandatory concept the rubric designates.
	score = 0.15 S1 + 0.35 S2,eff + 0.50 R	(1)

8.2 Reinforcement Learning Methodology
Difficulty adaptation is set up as a discrete-time, finite-horizon Markov Decision Process. Each turn works the same way: the agent looks at a state capturing how the candidate is doing both on content and on delivery, picks one action from the discrete action space, and gets a scalar reward back. Once the candidate answers whatever question that action produced, the episode moves on to the next state, and this repeats until a fixed maximum number of questions is reached. What the policy is actually optimising for, across all of that, is expected cumulative discounted reward over the episode.

Reward function (training-time, cited as existing evidence, not reproduced or altered in this phase). The reward combines five terms. Decision alignment with a hand-coded rule oracle carries the heaviest weight (0.6). An outcome-delta term rewards score improvement over the previous turn (0.3). A concept-breadth shaping bonus and a repetition penalty round out the picture, the latter discouraging the policy from collapsing onto one repeated action. Finally, a premature-escalation penalty applies whenever the policy selects Harder while the candidate's current score sits below 0.35.
Training provenance (per this project's own canonical evidence record, not independently retrained or altered in this phase). Training used Proximal Policy Optimisation [16] via Stable-Baselines3 (MlpPolicy, two 64-unit hidden layers), for 24,576 timesteps per training seed. Training seeds were {42, 123, 456, 789, 999}, evaluation seeds {1001, 2002, 3003, 4004, 5005}. The production checkpoint deployed in the live system is seed 123. This corrects the Phase-2 report's claim of "500,000 steps" and seeds (42, 123, 777), which do not trace to any stored artifact (Chapter 10, Section 10.2 documents this verification in full). If the trained checkpoint is not found at startup, a rule-based heuristic fallback activates automatically: score ≥ 0.75 → Harder, score ≥ 0.50 → Same, score ≥ 0.35 → Hint-equivalent Same, score < 0.35 → Easier, using the current score alone. It is deliberately simple, built only to keep the session functional, not to replicate the trained policy's more nuanced behaviour.
The project's own prior frozen research evidence (cited here, not reproduced) already established something important. Most of what looks like the PPO policy's contribution to tracking accuracy is really the guardrail layer's doing, once that layer is applied on top of it. The project's evidence record has, accordingly, explicitly withdrawn the claim that "PPO improves tracking," in favour of the more precise finding that guardrails account for essentially all of the measured improvement over a fixed-difficulty baseline. Both this phase's own RL testing (Chapter 10, Section 10.3) and its later extension's pre-registered confirmatory test (Chapter 10, Section 10.19) are consistent with that finding.
8.3 Post-Inference Guardrail Methodology
Guardrails run in a fixed, documented priority order (full conditions and rationale in Chapter 6, Section 6.3), built around an explicit pedagogical priority. A candidate who is both struggling and anxious (G4) is in the most vulnerable state and gets top priority. Escalating a strong candidate (G6) is the least urgent correction of the six, since delaying an escalation costs less than escalating too early or leaving anxiety unaddressed. This phase's testing found this priority structure sound but found and closed a boundary gap. The gap sat between the lowest-performance guardrails (G4/G1, triggering below 0.30) and the partial-understanding guardrail (G5, originally triggering only above 0.40). A below-average-scoring candidate sitting in that gap band could have had difficulty escalated by the raw policy with no safety check in place. The fix, widening G5's lower bound to 0.30, is documented with full rationale and verification in Chapter 10, Section 10.3.
8.4 Audio Analysis Methodology
Each voice recording passes through four parallel sub-pipelines that together produce a set of prosodic and linguistic features. These are aggregated into two scalar signals, confidence_score and hesitation_score, which the RL state builder and the FeedbackAgent both consume.
Transcription and pause alignment. First stop for the recording is OpenAI Whisper (or faster-whisper), which transcribes it with forced word-level alignment and hands back word tokens individually timestamped at start and end. Gaps between consecutive words that run longer than a minimum duration threshold count as pauses, and the pipeline keeps a running tally of both how many there were and how long they added up to.
Prosodic feature extraction. Separately, Librosa works on the raw waveform itself at the frame level: the mean and standard deviation of pitch off the fundamental-frequency contour, the mean and standard deviation of short-time energy, and a speaking-rate figure in words per minute computed from those same word-level timestamps.
Linguistic analysis. The transcript text itself gets scanned for signs of uncertainty and hesitation in the language. One count is filler words against a fixed list (um, uh, like, so, basically, right, you know); a separate density figure covers hedging phrases — "I think," "maybe," "probably," "I believe" — as a share of all sentences. Response length in words gets tracked too, alongside these, as one more supporting signal.
Hesitation and confidence aggregation. Three normalised quantities feed the hesitation score — pause count, filler-word rate, uncertainty-marker density — combined as a weighted sum that climbs as any of the three climbs. Confidence then runs the other way: it's derived as an inverse of that hesitation score, with the prosodic energy and pitch-stability signals adjusting it further.
Graceful degradation. If the audio pipeline is unavailable, say the candidate submits a text-only response or the pipeline simply fails, both the confidence score and hesitation score default to a neutral 0.5. That keeps the RL state vector well-defined and stops the policy from acting on a signal that was never actually measured. The FeedbackAgent, for its part, suppresses communication-analysis output instead of reporting metrics it cannot back up (Chapter 6, Section 6.4). Word-error-rate accuracy of the underlying transcription is not independently evaluated in this project, since no verified reference-transcript dataset exists. We state this as a methodological limitation that this phase's testing does not resolve. Chapter 10 confirms the pipeline runs correctly end-to-end, but it does not and cannot claim transcription accuracy.

Chapter 9: Implementation and Pseudocode
9.1 Orchestrator Implementation Decisions
This phase's inspection verified the implementation patterns in agents/orchestrator/interview_orchestrator.py detailed in Chapter 6, Section 6.1; they are summarised here as the basis for the pseudocode below:
Concurrency safety via `asyncio.Lock`: one orchestrator instance exists per WebSocket session, and every public mutating method grabs an instance-scoped lock at entry. Duplicate or out-of-order events within a single session get serialised this way, with no performance cost to other sessions.
Dependency injection for testability: the orchestrator's two most complex dependencies, the evaluator function and the question-selection function, come in as constructor arguments instead of being imported directly. That lets the 25-file automated test suite (Chapter 10) inject deterministic mocks and exercise every logical branch without ever starting an external service.
Single source of truth for state: all mutable session state lives in one dictionary. REST-facing code only ever receives a shallow copy of it. That keeps the serialisable state clean while internal bookkeeping (the question queue, attempt counts) stays private.
Idempotent termination: the session-termination method caches its generated report the first time it runs, so every call after that returns the cached result instead of regenerating the report from scratch.

9.4 Backend Dispatch: WebSocket Event Routing
This phase's verification confirms the backend dispatcher (apps/backend/main.py) contains no domain logic at all: it is a thin router that forwards each JSON-encoded WebSocket event to the matching orchestrator method:
async function websocket_handler(websocket, session_id):
    orch = session_registry[session_id]
    for message in websocket.iter_json():
        event = message["type"]
        if event == "start": await orch.start()
        elif event == "voice_answer": await orch.handle_voice_answer(
            message["transcript"], message["qid"], message["attempts"])
        elif event == "code_submission": await orch.handle_code_submission(
            message["code"], message["qid"], ...)
        elif event == "next_question": await orch.handle_next_question()
        elif event == "request_hint": await orch.request_hint(message["qid"])
        elif event == "skip_question": await orch.skip_question(message["qid"])
        elif event == "end_session":
            report = await orch.end()
            await websocket.send_json({"type": "session_report", "data": report})

Look closely and every one of those branches is nothing more than a method call handed off to the orchestrator — no conditional logic sits here, no state gets inspected, no score gets computed. That's intentional: it's what lets the dispatcher layer get tested, or swapped out entirely, without anyone having to touch interview domain logic.
9.5 React Frontend Implementation
Under the hood this is a fairly ordinary React-on-Vite app, split across two main views — the Interview Room and the Session Report — that both stay connected to the backend over the same single persistent WebSocket opened when the session starts.
Interview Room (`InterviewRoom.jsx`). Voice capture goes through the browser's own MediaRecorder API. Once submitted, the audio blob gets base64-encoded and shipped to the backend inside the voice_answer WebSocket message, riding alongside whatever transcript the browser's SpeechRecognition API already produced on its own — that transcript is there purely as a fallback, in case server-side Whisper transcription ends up slower than expected. Code questions get a CodeMirror editor instead, which only shows up when the question's own metadata says it's a coding question. Between questions, a difficulty indicator (1–5) ticks to whatever value the orchestrator sent in its next-question message. And if a hint ever gets requested — by the candidate, or unprompted by the guardrail/feedback layer — the orchestrator's Qwen-generated hint text renders inline, visually set apart so it reads as supplementary guidance rather than part of the question itself.
Session Report (`Report.jsx`). Once the orchestrator acknowledges that the session has ended, this view renders five things at once: a score-timeline chart tracking the candidate across every question, a per-topic bar chart calling out where they were weakest, a concept-coverage summary colour-coded by status (fully covered, partially covered, missed), a behaviour-metrics panel with average confidence, hesitation, and a clarity rating built from filler-word rate and response length, and a per-question drill-down that opens into the full fifteen-field feedback dictionary for that turn. Of everything the candidate sees, that drill-down is the most detailed — it's meant to be the thing they actually use to revise afterward.
9.6 Logger Implementation: Session Persistence
Running quietly alongside everything else, the Logger writes turn-level and session-level data to disk without ever blocking the evaluation or feedback pipeline it's shadowing. It's built to be strictly passive — it watches and records, but never feeds anything back into session state or any downstream decision. One consequence of that design: a logging failure can't touch the candidate's actual experience, and the logger itself can be switched off entirely in test environments with zero effect on how the rest of the system behaves.
Turn-level logging. After every evaluated turn, a structured log record holding that turn's complete state is appended as a single JSON object to a session-specific turns.jsonl file (one line per turn). JSONL was chosen over a single JSON array specifically because it supports incremental writes. Each turn flushes to disk immediately after evaluation. So a session that terminates unexpectedly, whether from a crash or a disconnect, leaves behind a recoverable partial log, not a corrupt or empty file. Each turn record carries the question ID and metadata, the candidate's full transcript or submitted code, all fifteen FeedbackAgent output fields, the raw and weighted component scores, the action the strategy layer selected, the resulting difficulty, and the audio-analysis features where available.
Session-level summary. When the session ends, a session-level aggregate file (summary.json) is generated once from the complete turns.jsonl record. It captures total questions attempted, per-topic average scores, the overall session mean score, the complete difficulty trajectory, the action-frequency distribution, average confidence and hesitation, and a final performance classification.
Graceful degradation. If the Logger fails to write to disk, say a filesystem permission error or disk-space exhaustion, the failure is caught, a structured warning goes to the application log, and the session simply continues. The session report still gets generated from in-memory session state and returned to the candidate. Only the persistent disk record is lost.

10.3 Guardrail Verification and the G5 Coverage Gap
Objective: confirm the six documented post-inference guardrail rules fire exactly where their stated conditions specify.
Procedure: 11 constructed states, covering each rule's documented trigger boundary plus the Section 10.2 observation, called directly against the real apply_canonical_guardrails() function (no mocking).
Result (before fix): the 10 originally-documented boundary cases all behaved exactly as expected. What didn't was the Section 10.2 observation (perf=0.35) — testing it directly exposed a real gap. None of the five documented rules reaches performance scores in the interval [0.30, 0.40) once the raw policy is proposing an escalation and confidence/hesitation sit in the moderate range, which meant a below-average-scoring candidate's difficulty could climb with nothing there to stop it.
Diagnosis: G4 and G1 only fire below perf < 0.30, while G5 (the partial-understanding rule, which holds difficulty at "Same") originally only picked up above perf > 0.40. Nothing covered the band in between — a gap in where the boundaries were drawn, not a bug inside either rule individually.
Fix applied: G5's lower bound widened from perf > 0.40 to perf >= 0.30, in rl/guardrails.py, a single-condition, minimally scoped change. This closes the gap exactly, without altering any other rule's condition, priority order, or returned action.
Verification: rerunning the 11-case test plus two new boundary cases gave 13/13 cases passing, zero mismatches. A full regression-suite rerun (Section 10.11) showed zero new failures.
Scope note, stated for reproducibility integrity: this fix only changes what rl/guardrails.py does going forward — it can't and doesn't reach back to alter any frozen research result already generated under the old guardrail version. There's a further wrinkle here, confirmed in this phase's later extension (Section 10.19) and then extended again by two more confirmed divergences this report's final correction pass turned up: the live InterviewOrchestrator runs its own private guardrail copy (interview_orchestrator.py::_apply_guardrails) for actual interview sessions. It's a separate, hand-duplicated version of the same six rules, and it never imports the canonical rl/guardrails.py module at all.
Comparing the two directly turns up three confirmed places where they disagree, none of which this report pushes into the live copy. The first is the G5 boundary just described: canonical now reads 0.30 <= perf < 0.65, while live still carries the original, unfixed 0.40 < perf < 0.65. The second is the G6 "gap" signal — canonical computes it as gap = perf - diff_norm (performance measured against the current question's difficulty), whereas live computes gap = perf - avg_perf (performance measured against the candidate's own rolling average), which is a genuinely different quantity, not a cosmetic rename. The third is the G6 nervous_expert condition: canonical defines it as a two-part disjunction, (0.80 < perf < 0.95 AND hes > 0.65) OR (conf < 0.40 AND hes > 0.60), but live only implements the second half of that — meaning live never suppresses a G6 escalation for a high-performing-but-hesitant candidate the way the canonical module's own documented rationale (Chapter 6, Section 6.3) says it should. All three stay on the record here as disclosed, unresolved engineering findings rather than being quietly patched as a side effect of writing this report.

10.20 Final Correction Pass: Three Code-Submission Defects Found and Live-Docker-Verified Fixed, and Documentation Corrections
Going back through this report section by section and cross-checking every claim against the current source code, a final correction pass turned up two related defects in the code-submission scoring path (fixed, Table 10.2 items 4-5) along with a set of documentation-only inaccuracies, each corrected at its own location in the relevant chapters and summarised here for traceability. That wasn't the end of it, though — a subsequent, independent re-verification pass over this same section found that item 4's own fix description had actually understated how bad things were, implying the primary WebSocket scoring path was unaffected when it actually carried a separate, more severe defect of the same family (item 5 below). A third pass then tried to live-verify items 4 and 5 against a reachable Docker daemon, and in the process found and fixed a third defect (item 6) that the first two passes' fixes had been quietly depending on without realising it. All three defects are fixed now, and — unlike what the first two passes claimed — actually exercised against a live Docker sandbox rather than left sitting as "specified but unverified."
Defect 1 (Table 10.2 item 4): `/api/run_code` always accepted code submissions regardless of correctness. This REST endpoint is a secondary, quick-run code-execution path, distinct from the primary WebSocket code_submission flow covered as Defect 2 below. It looked up the active question's test cases via getattr(sess, "_current_question", None). Since that attribute is never assigned anywhere on the session object, the lookup always returned None. That made test_cases always an empty list, which in turn made the endpoint's pass/fail check (passed_count == total_tests) vacuously true for zero executed tests. Every submission through this specific endpoint was reported as passing, regardless of whether the code was actually correct. The fix routes the endpoint through the orchestrator's existing _get_current_question() accessor. agents/coding_executor/coding_executor.py's is_accepted check now also requires total_tests > 0 before returning an "accepted" verdict, consistent with NFR-3's no-fabrication principle (Chapter 4, Section 4.3).
Defect 2 (Table 10.2 item 5): the primary WebSocket `code_submission` handler trusted client-supplied scoring fields with no server-side re-execution. This is the path that actually drives difficulty adaptation and session scoring for a candidate's coding answers, so the stakes here are higher. Unlike Defect 1, this handler did resolve the active question correctly via _get_current_question(), but only to look up metadata, not test cases. It never ran the sandbox itself. Instead it took passed, tests_passed, and tests_total straight from the client-sent WebSocket JSON payload (apps/backend/main.py, the code_submission branch). It fed them directly into orchestrator.handle_code_submission(), which uses them to compute the score and update session state. A client could send a WebSocket message claiming tests_passed == tests_total without the sandbox ever executing the submitted code. The system would accept it as genuine. That is an academic-integrity-relevant gap, not a cosmetic one. The fix has the handler resolve the active question's real test cases server-side (the same _get_current_question() accessor), and call the authoritative evaluate_c_submission() sandbox itself before scoring. passed/tests_passed/tests_total now come only from that server-side run; the client-sent values for these fields are no longer read at all.
Defect 3 (Table 10.2 item 6): found only while attempting to live-verify Defects 1 and 2, not by reading the report or the code in isolation. Driving a real Docker sandbox run against a real session surfaced the real problem. apps/backend/main.py's production question-bank loader, _load_question_bank_from_qns(), never attached a test_cases field to the question objects it builds from data/questions/qns.json. It copies roughly fifteen other fields per question but silently drops this one. Had this gone unnoticed, Defects 1 and 2's fixes would have been individually correct (require total_tests > 0, never trust the client) but jointly wrong in effect. Every real question's test_cases would still resolve to an empty list, so total_tests would always be 0. The new guard would then make every genuine code submission fail outright instead of being scored on its real correctness, swapping a false-accept defect for a false-reject one. A second, independent schema mismatch turned up at the same time. qns.json's raw test-case fields (input/expected_output/hidden) don't match the field names coding_executor.py reads (input/expected/is_hidden/is_mandatory). So even a naive fix that copied test_cases through unchanged would have left every expected-output comparison matching against an empty string. The fix normalises each question's test cases into the sandbox's expected schema at load time and attaches them to the question dict.
Verification status, stated plainly, for all three defects: this is now a live, Docker-verified result, not a specified-but-unverified one. With a reachable Docker daemon available, a real session was created through the actual FastAPI app (fastapi.testclient.TestClient, not a mock). Its question queue was inspected for a genuine coding question (C01, 4 real test cases, 2 public + 2 hidden). The following was then run end-to-end against the live Docker C sandbox:
REST `/api/run_code`, known-correct code: 3/4 tests passed, coding_score: 0.75, status: wrong_answer. This turned out to be a confirmed, precisely identified question-bank defect, upgraded from an earlier draft's vaguer "appears to have an inconsistent expected value." Question C01's hidden test case 4 (input: "5 8\n1 4 3 5 8") stores expected_output: "1 2". Re-deriving it by hand against the array [1,4,3,5,8], target 8: only indices (2,3) actually sum to the target (3+5=8). The stored pair (1,2) sums to 4+3=7, not 8. The mathematically correct expected output is `"2 3"`, not the stored "1 2". This is a confirmed error in data/questions/qns.json's own data, not a defect in the evaluation code. It still means a genuinely correct candidate solution is denied full marks by this one hidden test case. It is left uncorrected in the dataset as part of this report (a question-bank content change, scoped outside this correction pass without separate authorisation). It is flagged here so it isn't mistaken for a code defect or silently worked around.
REST `/api/run_code`, known-incorrect code (a stub that always returns -1 -1): 1/4 tests passed, coding_score: 0.25, correctly differing from the correct-code result.
WebSocket `code_submission`, the same incorrect code, submitted honestly (no forged fields): final_score: 0.25, tests_passed/tests_total: 1/4, passed: false, grade: F. This is the real sandbox result, flowing through difficulty-adaptation scoring exactly as intended.

WebSocket `code_submission`, the same correct code, submitted honestly: final_score: 0.65, tests_passed/tests_total: 3/4, consistent with the REST result. The score differs slightly from the raw 0.75 after the timing modifier, which is expected and separate from test-case scoring.
The full Docker-dependent unit-test batch (tests/unit/test_coding_executor.py, test_stage11_4_coding_verification.py) was re-run too, with the daemon reachable: 47 passed. Chapter 11: Conclusion and Future Work
11.1 Summary of Work Carried Out
Phase-2 delivered the implementation this phase builds on: a specialised, orchestrator-coordinated interview-preparation platform integrating RL-based difficulty adaptation, rubric-anchored semantic evaluation, prosodic audio analysis, and LLM-driven feedback generation. On top of that, this phase carried out a complete, evidence-graded verification and extension of PrepAIred across several stages. It began with a project-specific test strategy spanning 22 testing categories plus dedicated ML and RL evaluation. The existing 25-file/28-file automated test suite then ran to a final result of 264/283 (93.3%) passing, with every remaining failure fully attributed. Eleven newly authored, independently executed test scripts followed. These covered RL-policy sanity, guardrail-boundary verification, API contracts, adversarial input handling, database integrity, security configuration, compatibility, and structured UI inspection. Eight genuine defects were diagnosed and corrected across the full phase: a guardrail coverage gap, a platform-specific launcher crash, three further defects found in this phase's later extension, and three related code-submission defects found and live-Docker-verified in this report's final correction pass. Each was fixed with minimal, scoped changes and independently verified. Five were verified via the original unit-test suite; the remaining three (Chapter 10, Section 10.20) via a live, reachable-Docker end-to-end run. This phase also identified and corrected nine factual discrepancies between the Phase-2 report and the verified current implementation, and closed the previously-open sandbox-execution test gap through a live-Docker-daemon verification. It also ran an independent architecture audit, alongside real, stored RL/guardrail ablation and evaluator human-correlation evidence that now replace two of the Phase-2 report's unverifiable headline figures.
11.2 Verified Outcomes
The system's core technical contributions are confirmed functioning as designed: rubric-anchored multi-component semantic evaluation, RL-driven adaptive difficulty constrained by deterministic pedagogical guardrails, structured per-turn feedback, isolated code execution, and multi-service graceful degradation. That should not be read as "every defect found in this phase is fixed and closed," though it comes close. All eight defects found across this phase are fixed and independently verified: five via the original unit-test suite, and the remaining three (Chapter 10, Section 10.20) via a live, reachable-Docker end-to-end run covering real submissions through both the REST and WebSocket code-submission paths. The obs[4] test failure and the three live/canonical guardrail divergences (Section 11.4 below) are different in kind. They are disclosed, unresolved findings by design, not defects awaiting a fix this report withheld. Of the project's 12 original stated objectives (Chapter 10, Section 10.14), ten are fully met by direct, current evidence (two with corrected descriptions relative to the Phase-2 report). One, the Phase-2 abstract's specific "93.3% baseline calibration" figure, is confirmed unverifiable against any stored artifact. It has been replaced with a genuine, stored evaluator-correlation result (ρ=0.38). The last, sandboxed code execution, was initially blocked on an external environment dependency, and was later confirmed at the execution level once a Docker daemon became available.
A second, differently-scoped architecture audit independently confirmed something this phase did not simply take on faith from repeating the Phase-2-vs-code reconciliation. PrepAIred really is accurately described as a specialised, orchestrator-coordinated architecture, not a multi-agent system with autonomous, negotiating components. That finding is not a downgrade to the project, either. The audit also found that this architecture's own decomposition into independently-callable components, the learned policy and the deterministic guardrail layer in particular, is exactly what made it possible to run a rigorous, pre-registered test attributing the system's adaptive behaviour to one specific component, not the architecture as an undifferentiated whole. That is a genuine methodological benefit of specialisation. It is reported honestly here, alongside the equally honest finding that the learned policy's own marginal contribution is not statistically distinguishable from zero under the deployed guardrail shield.
11.3 Main Contributions of This Phase
A testing and evaluation package (testing_and_evaluation/) built specifically for this project and reproducible end to end — nothing in it that doesn't trace back to a stored log, a script, or a git commit.
Eight real defects, found and actually fixed rather than just written up, across the whole phase. They span a pedagogical-safety gap in the guardrail layer, a platform-compatibility crash in the application launcher, an LLM-output-parsing defect, lost session history on the admin dashboard, an inconsistency in a dormant code path's threshold, and three related code-submission defects uncovered in Chapter 10, Section 10.20 — a test-case lookup problem in the /api/run_code endpoint, a worse version of the same family of bug in the primary WebSocket code_submission handler (it trusted client-supplied pass/fail results instead of re-running the code server-side), and a third defect that only surfaced while live-verifying the first two, where the production question loader was never actually attaching real test cases to question objects. All three of those are fixed now and verified live against Docker.
A full reconciliation of this project's prior documentation against what the implementation actually does, with nine specific factual discrepancies corrected on direct evidence rather than assumption, and two headline figures that couldn't be verified replaced with real, stored numbers instead.
A scope finding this phase can stand behind precisely because two different inspection methods reached it independently: a separate, supplied research-paper draft describing a multi-agent coordination layer turns out not to exist anywhere in the codebase, which keeps that unverified material out of this report and any deliverable built alongside it.
A live-Docker-daemon verification that closes a test gap at the execution level that had been open until now, plus a fully specified research design — not yet run — for a future architectural comparison against a fair single-controller baseline.
11.4 Limitations
Carried forward, unresolved, and newly identified limitations are stated plainly, consistent with the evidence discipline applied throughout this report:
The RL evidence is synthetic, simulation-only. Both the deployed PPO policy's training and the project's prior frozen research results come from simulation, so nothing here claims anything about how a real candidate would actually learn.
There's still no formal human-subject usability or learning-outcome study. The Phase-2 report flagged this as the biggest outstanding evidentiary gap already, and it still is.
Speech-transcription accuracy hasn't been independently measured. This project has no verified reference-transcript dataset, so the audio pipeline's claim is limited to functional coverage — that it runs, not how accurate it is.
The live orchestrator's guardrail copy diverges from the canonical, research-evaluated module in three confirmed ways, not one. These are the G5 boundary (pre-fix 0.40 < perf < 0.65 live vs. fixed 0.30 <= perf < 0.65 canonical), the G6 "gap" signal (perf - avg_perf live vs. perf - diff_norm canonical), and the G6 nervous_expert condition (missing its first disjunct in the live copy) (Chapter 6, Section 6.3; Chapter 10, Section 10.3). All three are disclosed, not corrected, in this report.
One pre-existing, documented test failure stays unresolved, by design, until someone decides how to actually interpret it. Going back and re-verifying this turned up more ambiguity than the single failing test suggests. build_rl_observation() (agents/strategy/hybrid_orchestrator.py:116-147) normally computes obs[4] ("progress, t/T") from session["progress"], which is set and current in any ordinary live session, updated every turn by the orchestrator. Only if progress, turn_progress, and turn_idx/current_turn are all missing does it quietly fall back to session["last_time_norm"] — a completely different field tracking per-answer response time, not progress at all. That fallback path is exactly what the failing test is exercising. This report isn't claiming the fallback actually fires in ordinary live sessions, where progress is set directly anyway — but its existence, and the mismatch between what it measures and what obs[4] is documented to mean, hadn't been spelled out at this level of detail before, and the code itself still hasn't been touched.
Three `FeedbackAgent` helper methods sit there as dead code. _strong_points(), _detect_incorrect(), and _improvement_tips() are all defined, and none of them is ever called — the live feedback fields they were meant to populate come from the evaluator's own output instead (Chapter 6, Section 6.4). The candidate still gets those fields in practice; it's just that the specific methods this report's design documentation points to aren't the ones actually producing them.
The human-correlation evidence's gold-label construction carries a documented limitation — worth correcting here, since an earlier draft wrongly claimed this was undocumented. The three raters' scores got combined by taking the mean for 54 of the 64 items, and by adjudication for the other 10, where the spread exceeded 0.20. Reliability numbers look strong on paper (ICC(2,1)=0.9528, Krippendorff's α=0.9523), but most of that reflects how the benchmark's categories were structured rather than independent agreement on genuinely ambiguous cases (Chapter 10, Section 10.19). Who the raters and adjudicators actually were, their qualifications, and any institutional review process — none of that is documented in the repository.
The CrossEncoder component's training provenance remains incomplete, as already disclosed in this project's existing evidence record.
Substring-based misconception detection (inherited from Phase-2) has high precision but low recall, as the Phase-2 report itself already disclosed.
The proposed architectural comparison (Chapter 10, Section 10.19) is a design only. No single-controller baseline exists, and no comparative result has been collected.
11.5 Future Work
1. Resolve the guardrail-implementation divergence (Chapter 6, Section 6.3; Chapter 10, Section 10.3; Section 11.4; all three confirmed differences: the G5 boundary and the G6 gap/nervous_expert definitions). Either have the live orchestrator import the canonical rl/guardrails.py module directly, or formally decide to keep the two copies independent and port all three fixes to the live copy. This is a decision this phase deliberately left open instead of resolving unilaterally.
2. Resolve the one pre-existing known test failure (test_coding_turn_preserves_speech_confidence_and_does_not_fabricate_metrics); this phase deliberately deferred the decision instead of resolving it unilaterally.
3. Execute the proposed architectural comparison (Chapter 10, Section 10.19): build the specified single-controller baseline in an isolated research module, run the specified 17-category benchmark, and report the pre-specified primary and secondary endpoints. None of this was done in this phase, by design, pending explicit approval.
4. Locate or correct the two remaining unverified capstone-report figures not fully investigated in this phase (the Chapter-6 evaluator-calibration/CrossEncoder-fine-tuning experiment numbers referenced in the Phase-2 report), per the discrepancy list in Chapter 10, Section 10.12.
5. A formal, preregistered human-subject evaluation, as both the Phase-2 report and this phase's testing programme independently converge on identifying as the most valuable next step for strengthening this project's evidentiary base beyond automated/synthetic testing.
6. A real word-error-rate evaluation of the speech pipeline, requiring a newly constructed, verified reference-transcript dataset not currently available in this project.
7. Consider, as a separate, explicitly-scoped and preregistered future effort (not undertaken in this phase), whether to actually build the multi-agent "bounded-authority coordination" layer described in the supplied research-paper draft (Chapter 10, Section 10.13) for real. Doing so would make that paper's analytical framing genuinely applicable to this project, instead of requiring its exclusion.
11.6 Closing Remarks
This phase ran on one central discipline: every claim in this report, and in the project's broader documentation, should trace to a verifiable, stored piece of evidence. That discipline produced concrete, useful outcomes beyond simple test-count accumulation. It caught and fixed eight real defects across the phase before they reached a reader or a future developer unaware of them. It corrected nine factual inaccuracies in this project's own prior documentation before they could propagate further into a final submission, a research paper, or a presentation. It also caught a further set of this report's own inaccuracies in a final correction pass (Chapter 10, Section 10.20). It closed a previously-open execution-level test gap once the environment allowed, and replaced two previously-unverifiable headline figures with real, stored, honestly-caveated evidence. This is offered as the primary methodological contribution of this phase, independent of and complementary to the verified technical contributions of the underlying system itself. It also sets the plan of work this project carries forward into any subsequent phase: resolve the guardrail divergence, execute the proposed architectural comparison, and close the remaining disclosed gaps above.
