# Mentor justification — how the PrepAIred project became three papers (2026-09-21)

This is written to be said out loud. Every statement below is backed by files in the repository; where something is still open, it says so.

## A. The 30-second version
PrepAIred started as one large capstone system for technical-interview practice: an answer evaluator, a coding sandbox, local model feedback and an adaptive difficulty controller. The capstone paper described the whole system. When we audited it, we found that different parts needed different kinds of evidence, so we split the work into three papers that each ask one question and only report what the frozen results support. Paper 1 asks what we can actually show about the sandbox, the model-text boundary and failure handling. Paper 2 asks how well the evaluator agrees with human reference scores; it is on hold because the rating records are incomplete. Paper 3 asks what a learned difficulty controller adds over a constant one when both run under the same guardrail, in simulation only. Paper 3 goes to HCI International 2027 and Paper 1 to IEEE ICETC 2026.

## B. The 1-minute version
The original paper tried to say everything about the system at once, and some of its numbers came from early stages of the project. During the audit we separated what was measured from what was only designed. That left three questions with three different kinds of evidence.

Paper 1 is a dependability study: we ran nine fixed attack programs against the code sandbox, injected faults, and checked whether model-written feedback changes the score fields, using host-side observables and weakened controls. It claims only what those tests show, in one environment.

Paper 2 is an exploratory measurement of the answer evaluator against human reference scores on 64 constructed answers. The agreement is modest, and the paper says why it cannot be read as validation. I have put it on hold because the ethics, consent and rater records that a venue would ask for are not documented, and I will not fill those gaps by writing something we cannot support.

Paper 3 is a controlled simulation study. We compare a reinforcement-learning difficulty controller with a controller that always says "keep the same difficulty", both under the same rule-based guardrail. The result is an equivalence within a margin we chose ourselves, and it says nothing about real learners.

## C. The detailed explanation

**Why one project became several papers.** The capstone system has several components, and a single paper cannot give each of them a fair test. A claim about the sandbox needs attack programs and host-side checks. A claim about the evaluator needs human reference scores. A claim about the difficulty controller needs a comparison design with a stated null. Putting them in one paper forced weak evidence for one part to sit beside strong evidence for another. Separating them lets each paper state its own scope and limits.

**What each paper asks.**
- *Paper 1 (systems, dependability, containment):* which containment, authority-boundary and failure-handling properties of an LLM-assisted technical-assessment pipeline can be demonstrated under a specified environment? We tested nine fixed attack programs with five repetitions each, seven permissive controls that show the checks can detect a breach, a benign program, two fault scenarios that exposed real defects on the baseline system, and a fixed-turn test of whether model text reaches the score fields. Everything is one Windows/WSL2/Docker setup. One result I would point out is the ptrace case: the outcome came from a literal text filter in the application, not from kernel-level containment, and the paper says so. Another is that the executor's status string could not tell contained from breached runs for five of the seven attacks that had a control, so the deciding evidence is host-side.
- *Paper 2 (evaluator measurement):* how does the composite evaluator agree with human reference scores and with simpler baselines, and where does it err systematically? On 64 constructed answers to 8 questions, the Spearman correlation is 0.3812 with three separately named intervals. Using only the cross-encoder component, or that plus the semantic-similarity component, has higher point estimates than the full composite, though the intervals for the differences include zero. A word-count baseline is competitive, which shows the benchmark's categories differ in length. The evaluator under-scores correct answers on average.
- *Paper 3 (controlled simulation and equivalence):* under an identical guardrail, what does a learned PPO controller add beyond a state-blind constant policy? The persona-level difference in tracking error is −0.0350 with an interval of −0.0818 to +0.0021, inside a ±0.12 margin. That margin was chosen by us and has no external justification. Equivalence is not "no difference": the policies differ in their action sequences and in volatility. The guardrail activated on 563 of 1250 turns but changed the action on only 99, so I do not call activations "interventions".

**Why Paper 2 is on hold.** The scientific content is complete as an exploratory study, but a venue would expect an ethics statement, a consent statement, and documentation of who the raters and the adjudicator were and how independent they were. Those records are not in the repository. I can say what is missing; I cannot create it after the fact. The cross-encoder's training provenance is also incomplete. So the paper stays exploratory and unsubmitted until either the existing records are found or a new, properly documented rating round is run, which would be new work.

**Why Paper 3 is a simulation and equivalence study.** We have no real candidates and no learning outcomes, so any statement about real interview learning would be unsupported. The design isolates one thing: with the same guardrail on both sides, does the learned policy do better than a constant one on a pre-specified endpoint? The answer is that it is equivalent within our margin, and superiority was not met. That is informative for how adaptive systems are evaluated, and the paper stops there.

**Why Paper 1 is a systems evaluation.** Its evidence is about behaviour under stated conditions, not about a general guarantee. The tests are repeatability checks with fixed programs, so no inferential statistics are attached.

**Why we do not reuse the old paper's experimental claims.** The old paper is kept as a historical reference for terminology and formatting. The new papers cite only results that are frozen and traceable to stored files, and several early numbers from the project were superseded during the audit, for example an early pilot with a single rater. The old paper's headline figures are therefore not carried over; where a number appears in a new paper, it traces to a frozen result.

**What is frozen and what remains.** The results, protocols, checkpoints and benchmark files are frozen and were not changed. What remains is publication work: the HCII proposal is due 9 Oct 2026, the ICETC paper 10 Oct 2026, the HCII full paper 29 Jan 2027. Still needed from the authors: contact details, a decision on how to disclose AI assistance, and a few venue questions, such as whether ICETC wants an author-background page.

**Why the formats differ.** ICETC is IEEE double-blind with a two-column template and a five-page minimum. HCI International publishes with Springer, reviews single-blind, and asks first for an 800-word proposal. So the same science is packaged differently, and I do not force one format onto the other. For double-blind review there is an author-identifying master and a separate blinded copy, so that names, affiliations and file metadata cannot leak; the master is never uploaded.

**Why the old PrepAIred paper is a reference, not the source of truth.** It reflects an earlier stage of the system. The current source of truth is the audited repository record and the canonical evidence file. I use the old paper for the author list, the project vocabulary and the formatting style, and nothing else.

**If asked directly about tools.** AI assistance was used in this project, including for coding and for drafting and checking manuscript text, and Paper 1 states that an AI coding agent designed and ran its test campaigns. I am confirming the exact tools, versions and which sections they touched, and I will disclose them where each venue's policy requires.

## Short answers
**Why are there three papers from one project?** Because the system has three parts that need different evidence: a sandbox and pipeline evaluation, an evaluator measurement, and a controller comparison. One paper would have mixed strong and weak evidence.

**What is actually novel in each paper?** I do not claim new methods. Paper 1 gives a property-by-property account of what was and was not demonstrated for one pipeline, with oracles, controls and stated blind spots. Paper 2 gives an exploratory measurement of one evaluator with three separate intervals, a word-count baseline and a systematic under-scoring analysis. Paper 3 gives a matched comparison under an identical guardrail with a pre-specified equivalence rule and separate accounting for activations, action changes and no-ops. Related work already exists in each area, and the papers cite it.

**Why is Paper 2 on hold?** The rating, consent and ethics records are incomplete and cannot be reconstructed, and the evaluator's model provenance is partly unrecoverable. Venues will ask for these, so the paper stays exploratory and unsubmitted.

**Why Paper 3 to HCII and Paper 1 to IEEE ICETC?** Paper 3 is about evaluating adaptive instructional decisions, which is HCI International's Adaptive Instructional Systems area, and that venue starts with a proposal. Paper 1 is about the dependability of an assessment pipeline, which fits ICETC's assessment and education-technology tracks and its IEEE format. The fit is my judgement from the published scopes; neither venue has confirmed it, and nothing is submitted yet.
