**Title:** A Controlled Equivalence Study of Learned and Constant Difficulty Policies in Simulated Technical Interviews

<!-- PROPOSAL TEXT BEGINS -->

**Objective and significance.** Adaptive instructional systems often adjust question difficulty with a learned policy that sits behind a hand-written constraint layer. When the two are reported together, an observed outcome cannot be attributed to the learned component. This proposal reports a controlled, simulation-only evaluation of an adaptive difficulty policy used in technical-interview training. A proximal-policy-optimisation (PPO) controller and a state-blind Constant-Same policy run under an identical application-level rule-based guardrail, and the study asks what the learned controller adds beyond a constant action under the same constraints. The topic falls within evaluating adaptive instructional systems and adaptive instructional decisions. No learners took part, and the study does not evaluate learning effectiveness.

**Methods.** A simulated candidate has a persona type and a skill and produces performance, confidence, hesitation and response-time signals for the current difficulty. The controller observes a six-dimensional state and chooses among Easier, Same and Harder; difficulty lies between 1 and 5 and starts at 3.0. Forty authored personas (five types by eight skill levels) each have an authored target difficulty, and a session has ten turns. The primary endpoint is the persona-level difference in mean absolute error between session difficulty and target difficulty. Five frozen PPO checkpoints (five training seeds, each trained for 24,576 timesteps against a single default simulated candidate) were compared with Constant-Same over 20 evaluation seeds. The persona is the statistical unit and the training seed is a second random factor; the interval is a two-way cluster bootstrap (10,000 resamples). The equivalence margin of ±0.12 difficulty levels and a superiority threshold of −0.20 were author-selected; no external empirical justification was identified. The protocol and harness were committed and tagged in the project repository before the run; the tags are local and no external registry entry was made. Three further pre-specified analyses (persona-only bootstrap, seed-level t interval, leave-one-seed-out) give seven sensitivity estimates. A separate evaluation-only replay of the frozen checkpoints on five fixed personas (125 PPO sessions, 1250 turns) accounts for the guardrail and for behavioural divergence.

**Results.** The difference in tracking error (PPO minus Constant-Same) was −0.0350 (95% interval −0.0818 to +0.0021), classified equivalent within the margin; superiority was not met, and all seven sensitivity estimates were classified equivalent. Equivalence does not mean identical behaviour. In the replay, the guardrail activated on 563 of 1250 PPO turns (45.0%) and changed the selected action on 99 (7.9% of turns); 464 activations were no-ops, and 41 of 125 sessions contained at least one override. Relative to Constant-Same with the same guardrail, the final-action sequence differed in 50 of 125 sessions, the executed difficulty path in 25 and session tracking error in 5; these counts follow different definitions. Guarded PPO was more volatile than Constant-Same (difference +0.149, interval 0.067 to 0.254) and than unguarded PPO.

**Contributions and implications.** The study offers a matched-policy comparison in which the constraint layer is held fixed, an equivalence result with its pre-specified rule and margin, and a guardrail accounting that separates rule activations from action overrides and from attempted boundary actions. For work on evaluating adaptive instructional systems, the implication is methodological: report constraint-layer accounting and a matched simpler baseline before attributing an outcome to a learned policy, and read equivalence within a margin as a statement about that margin. The evidence stops at the simulator. The simulator, personas, target rule and margin are authored by the study team; the training reward contains an alignment term with an authored rule; the five checkpoints were trained against one simulated candidate, with training and evaluation settings that differ; no Elo, item-response or adaptive-testing baseline was run; and there are no real candidates and no learning outcomes. The full paper will state these limits alongside the results.
